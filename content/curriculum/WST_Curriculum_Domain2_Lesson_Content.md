## Domain 2 Overview

**Total estimated engagement time:** 8–10 hours across all four modules
**Prerequisites:** Domain 1 (Foundations)

Domain 1 told you what is happening in the world. Domain 2 puts you inside a specific firm, with specific people, who are living that world in real time — often afraid, often underprepared, often carrying more institutional weight than anyone in the building has ever named out loud.

This domain is where the WST practice is most distinct. Every other consulting framework either starts with the technology or treats people as the implementation risk to be managed after the real decisions are made. This curriculum starts with the people — completely, before anything else — because that is where the actual information is, and because it is the right thing to do.

The four modules build in a specific sequence. Module 2A establishes what human capacity is and how to audit it. Module 2B establishes the psychological safety conditions that allow the audit to happen honestly. Module 2C builds the workforce transition capability — the technically hardest and ethically most weighted work in the domain. Module 2D integrates all three into a coherent first-phase practice.

**The founding principle of this domain:**

Every person inside a business contributes something that AI and automation will never replicate. Judgment shaped by lived experience. Relationships built over time. Institutional memory. Creative synthesis. The capacity to hold complexity without reducing it to a rule. The practitioner's job is to find that contribution and elevate it — not to protect headcount, not to shield anyone from accountability, but because the contribution is real and because organizations that fail to see it make structurally worse decisions.

When a company cannot find a way to evolve someone's role toward something more human and more valuable as AI absorbs the task substrate beneath them, that is a failure of organizational imagination. Not an inevitability. The practitioner names it as such.

**The four modules:**
- **2A** — Human capacity and strengths-based work design
- **2B** — Team culture, psychological safety, and trust
- **2C** — Workforce transition: the hard skill and the ethical practice
- **2D** — The people audit in practice

# MODULE 2A — Human Capacity and Strengths-Based Work Design

**Estimated engagement time:** 2.0 hours

**Learning objectives:** By the end of this module, you can:

1. Conduct a human capacity audit — surfacing not just what a person's job description says but what they actually do, what institutional knowledge they carry, and what the organization would lose if they left tomorrow.
2. Run a strengths-and-crafting conversation with an individual employee that produces real insight about what role design would make them most effective and most engaged.
3. Apply job crafting theory to redesign a role around what a person does well and finds meaningful, rather than starting from a blank job description.
4. Distinguish the evidence-based tools (CliftonStrengths, job crafting) from their validity limits, and apply them as conversation-starters rather than final verdicts.

## Lesson 2A-1: What Human Capacity Actually Is

**Estimated time:** 30 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

Start with what the job description says. For most roles in most SMBs, that document is somewhere between incomplete and fictional — not because anyone is being dishonest, but because the actual work of knowing what to do, when to do it, and how to handle the situations that are not in the manual cannot be captured in a job description. It lives in the person.

This is what the research calls tacit knowledge. Michael Polanyi introduced the concept in the 1960s with a deceptively simple observation: we know more than we can tell. A seasoned operations coordinator does not just complete tasks; they recognize which tasks matter more than they appear, which client relationships need to be handled with care this week, which vendor is reliable and which one's promises should be cut in half. None of that is written down anywhere. All of it is real, and all of it is value.

Tom Davenport's work on knowledge workers extended this framing to the question of organizational performance. The argument is not just that tacit knowledge is humanly interesting — it is that it is a primary driver of performance. The difference between the operations coordinator who can do the job and the operations coordinator who runs the department is not the documented tasks. It is the undocumented judgment that accumulates over years of doing work in a specific context with specific people.

This matters for the AI moment in a precise way. What AI can absorb is the documented, repeatable task substrate of a role — the parts that can be specified, measured, and systematized. The Noy & Zhang writing productivity research, the customer service studies, the GitHub Copilot coding benchmarks all demonstrate genuine gains on tasks that have clear inputs and outputs. What they cannot touch is the judgment work: the pattern recognition that operates below the level of explicit rule, the relationship capital that is personal rather than institutional, the synthesis capacity that turns ambiguous inputs into good decisions.

When a client says, "We're going to automate this role," the question the practitioner asks is: which parts of this role? The answer is almost never the whole role. And what remains — after honest, rigorous analysis — is usually more valuable than what was automated, because it is the part that AI cannot do.

**The three categories of human capacity the practitioner is looking for:**

*Tacit knowledge and institutional memory.* What does this person know that is nowhere written down? This includes how things actually work (not how the SOP says they work), which relationships are load-bearing (even if they are not on an org chart), what has failed before (and why, in detail that a new hire would not have), and what the organization's rhythms and sensitivities are. This knowledge often walks out the door when a person leaves, and the organization does not realize what it lost until much later.

*Judgment and synthesis.* The capacity to take ambiguous, incomplete, or contradictory information and arrive at a good decision. This is not general intelligence — it is contextual intelligence. It is calibrated to this firm, these clients, these constraints. It cannot be transferred by handing someone a manual, and it cannot be replicated by a model trained on generic data. The practitioner's job is to recognize it when they see it and name it specifically.

*Relationship capital.* The trust that has been built, person to person, over time. In a 15-person firm, a significant portion of the client relationships are relationships with specific people, not with the firm as an entity. The account manager's clients are often loyal to the account manager. The vendor relationships that work well are often personal. The internal trust that allows honest conversation is often built around specific people. This is not a soft observation — it is a structural reality that has significant implications for any workforce transition.

**What the practitioner does with this:**

The human capacity audit is not a performance review. It is not an assessment of whether someone is doing their job correctly. It is an inventory of what exists — what is present in this person that the organization depends on, whether or not it has ever been named.

The output of the audit is not a rating. It is a clear description: here is what this person contributes that is not in their job description, here is what the organization would lose if they left, and here is what any role redesign or transition needs to account for.

**Key takeaway:** Human capacity is not what the job description says. It is what would disappear if the person did.

## Lesson 2A-2: Conducting the Human Capacity Audit

**Estimated time:** 40 minutes
**Method:** Reading + simulation

---

The human capacity audit is a structured conversation — not a survey, not a form, not a performance review session. It is a conversation designed to surface what the person actually does and knows, which is almost always different from what is documented anywhere.

**The four audit categories:**

*Documented tasks.* Start here because it establishes a shared baseline. What does the job description say? What does the SOP require? What shows up in their calendar and task list on a normal week? This is not where the interesting information is — but it is the foundation. You cannot see what is undocumented without first mapping what is documented.

*Undocumented tasks.* The gap between what the SOP says and what actually happens is the most important gap in the organization. Ask: "Walk me through last Tuesday — not the ideal version, but what actually happened." Ask about the last time something went wrong and what they did. Ask about the requests they get that are not in their job description. Ask about the things they do because no one else will. This category includes the informal coordination, the problem-solving, the relationship maintenance, and the judgment calls that happen continuously without ever being captured anywhere.

*Institutional memory.* Ask: "If you left tomorrow and couldn't tell anyone anything — what would break?" Ask what the new hire misses that takes a year to learn. Ask what the client relationships depend on that is personal to them rather than institutional. Ask what has been tried before that failed, and why — because that knowledge, specifically, prevents the organization from making the same mistake again. The answer to these questions is the person's institutional memory, and it is often astonishingly valuable relative to how visible it is.

*Informal contributions.* What holds the team together? What does this person do that shapes the culture, the communication, the morale — without it being in any job description? Who do people go to when they are stuck, even if that is not officially this person's role? What does the team know this person for that is different from what their title says? This category includes the trust-brokering, the culture-carrying, the informal mentorship, and the conflict-prevention that makes a small organization function.

**How to conduct the conversation:**

The goal is to surface real information, which means the person needs to feel safe enough to give it. Most employees in a small firm have learned, through experience, that being forthcoming about what they actually do — especially the undocumented parts — is sometimes met with puzzlement or, worse, reorganization that removes what they were doing. This is the psychological safety problem that Module 2B addresses directly. For now: establish from the outset that this conversation is about understanding their contribution, not assessing it.

The questions that open each category:

- "Take me through a typical week — not the ideal version, but what actually happens."
- "What do you do that isn't in your job description?"
- "If you left tomorrow, what would be hardest to replace?"
- "What do new people usually get wrong that you had to learn by experience?"
- "Who comes to you when they're stuck, and what for?"
- "What have you seen tried before that didn't work — and do you know why?"
- "What client or vendor relationships are partly built around your specific relationship with them?"

**Reading the conversation:**

Silence on a category is information. If someone has nothing to say about undocumented tasks, one of two things is true: either the role is genuinely fully documented and they work entirely within it (rare in an SMB of any health), or they have learned not to name the informal work because it has not been valued. Probe gently: "Most roles end up with some tasks that aren't officially part of the job — what about yours?" If still nothing, make a note and look for the undocumented work through observation rather than conversation.

Casualness is also information. When someone describes something significant — a client relationship they have built over years, a judgment call they make daily — with complete matter-of-factness, as though it is obviously part of the furniture, that is usually a signal that the contribution is both important and unrecognized. The practitioner names it back clearly: "So you're the primary relationship for those four accounts — does the organization have any of that relationship if you're not here?"

**The audit worksheet:**

The WST Audit Methodology (Section B2) includes the specific worksheet for conducting and documenting this conversation in an engagement. The structure is: documented tasks → undocumented tasks → institutional memory → informal contributions → summary (what would be lost, what remains, redesign direction). Use it as a field guide, not a rigid interview script.

**Simulation:**

You are meeting with Marcus, the Operations Coordinator at a 20-person professional services firm. His job description says: schedule client meetings, manage project trackers, coordinate vendor contracts, produce weekly status reports. His manager tells you, before the meeting: "Marcus is reliable. He keeps things moving. I'm not sure exactly what he does all day but things would break without him."

Design the conversation you will have with Marcus. Write the five questions you will prioritize, in order, and explain what information each one is designed to surface. Then write what you would consider a strong answer and a concerning answer for each question — strong meaning it indicates significant undocumented human capacity, concerning meaning it may indicate the role is more automatable than the manager's comment suggested.

*Debrief:* The manager's comment — "I'm not sure exactly what he does all day but things would break without him" — is one of the most reliable signals a practitioner can receive. It means the value is real and the visibility is low. The audit's job is to translate the invisible into the named.

**Key takeaway:** The questions that reveal the most are the ones about what would disappear if the person did — not what they do, but what would be lost.

## Lesson 2A-3: Strengths-Based Work Design — The Evidence Base

**Estimated time:** 30 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

The human capacity audit tells you what a person contributes. Strengths-based work design tells you how to build a role that gets the most from what they contribute. The two are not the same, but they work together.

**The CliftonStrengths research:**

The Gallup CliftonStrengths framework emerged from a multi-decade research program asking a deceptively simple question: what would happen if organizations focused on what people do well rather than fixing what they do poorly? The meta-analytic evidence, aggregated across hundreds of studies and millions of respondents, shows consistent effects on engagement and performance when people work in roles that use their natural strengths — effect sizes in the 0.2–0.4 range, which is meaningful for organizational interventions.

The academic psychometrics on CliftonStrengths are contested. The 34-theme taxonomy has been criticized as overlapping and insufficiently distinct. The instrument's test-retest reliability is adequate rather than strong. A practitioner who cites it as precision measurement will be challenged by anyone with a methods background. A practitioner who uses it as a structured conversation about what someone does well and finds meaningful — as a conversation-starter, not a verdict — will get genuine value from it in the field.

Use it as the latter. The assessment produces language that helps people articulate what they already know about themselves. That language is useful in a role redesign conversation. The 34 themes are not a scientific taxonomy of human ability — they are a vocabulary set that makes certain things easier to say.

**Job crafting theory:**

Amy Wrzesniewski and Jane Dutton introduced the job crafting concept in a 2001 paper that has since been replicated, extended, and applied across hundreds of contexts. The core observation: workers do not passively execute the tasks in their job description. They actively reshape their roles — adjusting which tasks they emphasize, how they relate to colleagues and clients, and how they think about the meaning of their work. This is not rule-breaking; it is how competent people in real organizations actually operate.

The research findings are consistent: workers who actively craft their roles report higher engagement, better performance, and greater sense of meaning than workers who perform their jobs as written. The mechanism is alignment — when the tasks a person actually does map onto their strengths and values, the work sustains rather than depletes.

For the practitioner, this has a specific implication. Role redesign is not just about removing automated tasks and assigning new ones. It is about identifying what the person has already been doing, unofficially, that maps to their strengths — and building the new role around that, with explicit permission and structure. The redesigned role often already exists in the person's informal practice. The practitioner's job is to make it visible and legitimate.

**The 2024–2026 AI-era job crafting research:**

The emerging work from Bindl and others suggests that workers are already crafting their roles in response to AI without formal permission or organizational design support. They are offloading the automatable tasks to AI tools on their own, creating space for more judgment-intensive and relationship-intensive work — and doing it quietly, often without disclosing the AI use. This is the BYOAI dynamic from Domain 1 showing up at the level of individual role design.

The implication for the practitioner: the workers who are thriving in the AI transition are often the ones who have already started redesigning their own roles. The practitioner's job is to find them, understand what they have figured out, and use that as the template for the broader redesign.

**Validity limits — be explicit about these:**

Both CliftonStrengths and job crafting have limits that the practitioner should name rather than paper over. CliftonStrengths is a personality-adjacent instrument with contested construct validity. Job crafting research is predominantly self-report and may overstate the degree of voluntary agency workers have in shaping their roles, particularly in lower-wage or higher-supervision environments. Neither tool is appropriate as a basis for consequential HR decisions. Both are appropriate as frameworks for structured conversation about what a person does well and what a role could look like if it were designed around that.

**Key takeaway:** Strengths-based work design is not about making people feel good. It is about building roles that get the most from what a person actually contributes — which is better for the person, better for the organization, and more likely to survive the AI transition intact.

## Lesson 2A-4: Running the Strengths-and-Crafting Conversation

**Estimated time:** 40 minutes
**Method:** Simulation

---

The strengths-and-crafting conversation is not an assessment administration. It is not a performance review. It is a structured conversation with a person about what energizes them, what they are good at that their current role does not fully use, and what the role would look like if it were designed around their best work.

**The conversation structure:**

*Opening:* Establish the purpose clearly. "I'm trying to understand what you do well and what kind of work you find most engaging — not to evaluate your performance, but to think about how your role might evolve as the organization changes. This isn't about what's in your job description; it's about you."

*Part 1 — Strengths:* "What work do you do where you find yourself in a kind of flow state — where time passes and you haven't noticed?" "What are you better at than most people you've worked with?" "What do colleagues come to you for that isn't officially your job?" "What part of your work do you find yourself doing well even on a bad day?"

These questions are designed to surface strengths through behavior rather than self-assessment. The person who says "I'm a people person" is telling you how they self-identify. The person who says "I've noticed that when a client is frustrated, I always seem to be able to get them talking again" is telling you what they actually do.

*Part 2 — Frustrations and friction:* "What parts of your current role feel like a drain — things you can do but that exhaust rather than energize you?" "What do you wish someone else would handle?" "If you could hand off one category of tasks, what would it be?"

This is where the automation conversation happens implicitly. The tasks that drain rather than energize are often exactly the tasks that AI can absorb — the repetitive, the mechanical, the information-processing work that requires accuracy but not judgment. When a person articulates their drains, they are often describing what should be automated, and the redesigned role is what remains.

*Part 3 — What the role could be:* "If your role were redesigned from scratch around what you do best, what would it look like?" "What work do you wish you were doing more of?" "What would make you feel like you were contributing at your best?"

This part requires the practitioner to listen without correction. The person's answer is a hypothesis, not a design spec. It will contain things that are organizationally impossible, things that are genuinely good ideas, and things that reveal what kind of role would actually retain and develop this person. The practitioner's job is to hear all of it without prematurely evaluating any of it.

*Closing:* Summarize what you have heard without reducing it. "Here's what I'm taking away from this conversation." Name the strengths you observed specifically. Name what you heard about the work that energizes versus drains. Name one or two directions for role redesign that seem worth exploring. Leave the person feeling that the conversation was about them, not about an organizational agenda.

**Writing the role redesign hypothesis:**

After the conversation, the practitioner writes a brief — not a recommendation, a hypothesis — for what this person's role could look like. The format: what the person contributes that AI cannot replicate; what the automated task reduction creates space for; what the redesigned role could be anchored to; what would need to change organizationally to make that redesign real.

This is a first draft, not a final recommendation. It gets refined through the workforce transition work in Module 2C and integrated into the people-layer summary in Module 2D.

**Simulation:**

You have conducted the strengths-and-crafting conversation with Elena, an Account Manager at a 14-person marketing agency. Here is what you heard:

- She lit up when talking about the strategy conversations with clients — the "what are we actually trying to accomplish here" conversations before the campaign work starts.
- She mentioned, with visible resignation, that she spends about 40% of her time on reporting — pulling data from multiple platforms, formatting it, sending it to clients who, she suspects, rarely read it in detail.
- She said the thing she wishes she were doing more of is "being in the room when the client is deciding what to do next — not just executing on what they've already decided."
- When asked what she is better at than most people she has worked with, she said: "I can tell when a client is about to churn before they say anything. I can't always explain it, but I'm almost never wrong."

Write the role redesign hypothesis for Elena. What does she contribute that AI cannot replicate? What does the automated reduction of reporting work create space for? What could her redesigned role be anchored to? What would the organization need to change to make it real?

*Debrief:* Elena's churn-prediction observation is the most important thing she said. It is a precise description of tacit knowledge — pattern recognition built from experience, operating below the level of explicit rule, calibrated to a specific client base. That capacity is not in any job description and is not automatable. The redesigned role builds around it.

**Key takeaway:** The strengths-and-crafting conversation is not an assessment. It is a listening exercise designed to surface what a role could become if it were built around what the person actually does best.

**Module 2A Assessment**

**Type:** Role audit + redesign brief

**Prompt:** You are given a profile of an employee at a fictional 20-person operations firm: job title (Operations Coordinator), a list of documented tasks, three observations from their manager, two observations from a colleague, and a brief self-assessment the person wrote.

*(a)* Conduct a written human capacity audit for this person using the four-category framework (documented tasks, undocumented tasks, institutional memory, informal contributions). Be specific — name what you are inferring from the evidence and what you would need to verify through direct conversation.

*(b)* Write a 150-word role redesign brief: what this person's role could look like if it were anchored in their human capacity and the tasks that AI cannot replicate. Be explicit about what the automated task reduction creates space for.

## Lesson 2B-1: What Psychological Safety Is — and What It Is Not

**Estimated time:** 25 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

Amy Edmondson's research program on psychological safety began in hospital wards in the 1990s, where she found a result that was initially counterintuitive: the teams that reported more medical errors were not the ones making more mistakes. They were the ones where it was safe to report errors when they occurred. The relationship between safety and performance was the inverse of what naive inspection suggested.

Edmondson defined psychological safety precisely: the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Not the absence of conflict. Not harmony. Not comfort. Safety, specifically, for the kinds of interpersonal risks that allow a team to function well — asking a question that might make you look uninformed, raising a concern that might be unwelcome, naming a problem before it becomes a crisis, disagreeing with someone who has more power.

The meta-analytic evidence is consistent across organizational contexts and has been replicated across industries, firm sizes, and cultures: teams with higher psychological safety show better learning behavior, better error detection and correction, and better performance on complex tasks. The effect is particularly strong on tasks that require genuine collaboration — where information needs to flow across boundaries, where errors need to be caught early, and where novel problems require honest input from multiple perspectives.

The 2024–2026 extensions of this research to AI-specific contexts are important for the practitioner. Two findings stand out. First: AI adoption in teams is suppressed when psychological safety is low. Workers who fear that disclosing AI use will be perceived as laziness, rule-breaking, or job-threat signaling do not disclose it — they use it covertly, without organizational awareness or governance. Second: job-threat environments specifically suppress the honest communication that AI transitions require. Workers who believe their role may be eliminated are less likely to report problems with AI tools, less likely to ask for help when they do not understand something, and less likely to raise concerns about AI decisions they believe are wrong.

This is the practical problem for the practitioner: the firms that most need honest information about how AI is affecting their teams are the firms where the safety conditions for that information to surface are most likely to be compromised.

**What psychological safety is not:**

It is not comfort. A psychologically safe team can have very difficult conversations — about performance, about failure, about disagreement. The safety is what allows those conversations to happen without the relationship collapsing.

It is not harmony. Teams with high psychological safety are often noisier and more openly in conflict than teams with low safety. The difference is that the conflict is productive — it surfaces real disagreement rather than suppressing it until it becomes a crisis.

It is not the absence of accountability. Edmondson distinguishes clearly between a culture of blame (where errors are punished and therefore hidden) and a culture of accountability with safety (where errors are named, analyzed, and corrected without punishment for honesty). The latter is the target. The former is what the practitioner almost always walks into.

**Edmondson's seven-item diagnostic scale:**

The research-based assessment asks team members to rate agreement with seven statements:

1. If you make a mistake on this team, it is not held against you.
2. Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues.
3. People on this team sometimes reject others for being different.
4. It is safe to take a risk on this team.
5. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help.
6. No one on this team would deliberately act in a way that undermines my efforts.
7. Working with members of this team, my unique skills and talents are valued and utilized.

Items 3 and 5 are reverse-scored. The seven items together produce a score that is interpretable in context — not as a number that means something absolute, but as a signal about where safety is relatively high or low across the team.

In practice, the practitioner rarely administers this as a formal survey. They use these seven dimensions as an observation guide — looking for behavioral signals of each dimension in how the team talks (or does not talk) in front of them, and using the items as conversation prompts when they need to probe directly.

**Key takeaway:** Psychological safety is what allows the team to tell you what is actually happening. Without it, you are auditing a performance rather than a reality.

## Lesson 2B-2: The Safety Paradox in AI Transitions

**Estimated time:** 25 minutes
**Method:** Reading + case analysis

---

This is the pattern that destroys trust fastest in an AI-era SMB engagement: a leader who is genuinely asking for honest team input about the AI transition — what is working, what is not, what people are worried about — while simultaneously running workforce planning that may eliminate some of those team members' roles, and not disclosing the latter to the team.

This is not usually malicious. The leader is often simultaneously uncertain about the workforce planning (they have not made decisions yet), feeling pressure to keep options open, and genuinely wanting the team's input on the AI side without wanting to trigger panic about the workforce side. These motivations are understandable. The effect is not.

When a team member gives honest feedback — "I'm worried about what this automation means for my job" — and the leader reassures them without disclosing that there is, in fact, an active conversation about their job, what breaks is not just trust in this leader about this issue. What breaks is the underlying safety assumption: that this is a team where honest expression is met with honest response. Once that assumption breaks, it does not come back easily.

The practitioner walks into this situation regularly. The signal is usually a gap between what the leader tells you about the team communication climate and what you observe in the team. The leader says: "We're really open with the team about what we're doing with AI." The team's body language in a group conversation says otherwise. The questions the team does not ask — about job security, about what the AI means for their roles — are as informative as the questions they do ask.

**How to address it with a client:**

This requires a direct conversation with the client before the engagement proceeds. Not as a lecture on transparency, not as a moral intervention, but as a practical problem: "I need to flag something I've observed, because it affects whether the information I gather is going to be useful. The team appears to be in a lower-disclosure mode than you're describing — they're not asking questions that, given what you've told me is happening, I would expect them to be asking. My working hypothesis is that there's a gap between what the team thinks is safe to say and what is actually true. Before I go any further, I want to understand whether there are any workforce planning conversations happening that the team doesn't know about — because if there are, we need to decide together how to handle that before we ask people to be open about the AI transition."

Most clients will tell you. Some will not. If they do not, the practitioner is working with information that is materially compromised — and needs to name that constraint clearly, both to the client and in the engagement findings.

**Composite teaching case:**

A 22-person logistics firm. The owner brought in a consultant to help design an AI-based dispatch system. He was simultaneously in conversations with a logistics software vendor about a managed service that would eliminate two of the three dispatch coordinator roles. He had not told the team. The consultant ran a discovery process asking staff for honest input about the dispatch workflow — what worked, what did not, where the pain points were.

The dispatch coordinators gave thorough, good-faith input. They also gave thoughtful suggestions about how to improve the AI tools — how to make them better, what human judgment the system would still need. Three months later, two of them were let go. The third, who stayed, stopped giving honest input to any consultant, any initiative, any change process for the remaining four years he was with the firm. The owner later reflected that he "lost the team" after that transition — that a kind of openness that had been there was gone.

What the owner experienced as an unfortunate but necessary business decision had, from the team's perspective, been a betrayal of the implicit contract of the discovery process. They had been asked to help design something that was being built, in part, to eliminate them — without their knowledge.

**Key takeaway:** The safety paradox is not a communication problem. It is a trust architecture problem. The fix is not better messaging — it is disclosure, in whatever form is appropriate, before you ask for honesty.

## Lesson 2B-3: Five High-Leverage Practices

**Estimated time:** 25 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

These are not the only ways to build psychological safety. They are the five practices that the research most consistently associates with safety in high-uncertainty environments — and the ones that translate most directly to the SMB context where the practitioner works.

**Practice 1 — Leaders go first.**

Edmondson's research is consistent: psychological safety is primarily a function of leader behavior. Leaders who model fallibility — who name their own uncertainty, who acknowledge what they do not know, who respond to mistakes with curiosity rather than blame — create the conditions for teams to do the same. In an AI transition specifically, the leader who says "I don't fully understand how this is going to work yet, and I want us to figure it out together" is building safety. The leader who projects complete confidence about a genuinely uncertain situation is, paradoxically, shutting it down.

In practice, this means the practitioner often works with the leader first. Not to script what they say, but to establish that honest uncertainty — "I don't know yet what this means for everyone's role" — is more safety-building than false reassurance. This is counterintuitive for many leaders, who believe that confidence is what the team needs. What the team usually needs is truth, even when the truth is uncertainty.

**Practice 2 — Frame AI as a design problem, not a performance evaluation.**

One of the fastest ways to collapse safety in an AI transition is to structure the conversation in a way that implies the AI is assessing the team's performance. "We're using AI to identify inefficiencies" sounds, to a team under job-threat stress, like: "We're using AI to find out who isn't working hard enough." Even if that is not the intent, the interpretation is entirely predictable.

The reframe: AI is being introduced to handle the parts of the work that do not require human judgment, so that the team can do more of the work that does. This shifts the implicit question from "who is not performing well enough to survive the AI review" to "what can we build together that uses both the AI and the team's capabilities better." The psychological impact is significant.

**Practice 3 — Maintain predictability.**

Uncertainty about what is going to happen is corrosive to safety. Workers who do not know what the AI transition means for their roles — and are not given a timeline for when they will know — are in a prolonged threat state that gradually makes honest communication impossible. The SCARF research (David Rock, NeuroLeadership Institute) identifies certainty as one of the primary psychological needs that, when threatened, activates the same neural threat response as physical danger.

The practitioner's guidance to clients: even bad news delivered on a predictable schedule is less damaging than uncertainty. "We will have a clear picture of what this means for everyone's roles by the end of Q3, and we will communicate it directly at that point" is safer — psychologically, for the team — than continued ambiguity, even if the news at Q3 is difficult. Predictability allows planning. Uncertainty produces anxiety that degrades performance and trust simultaneously.

**Practice 4 — Create a protected channel for AI concerns.**

In most SMBs, the channel for raising concerns is the same channel for everything else — a one-on-one with a manager, a team meeting, an informal conversation. In an AI transition, that channel is often compromised because the person most relevant to the concern (the manager making the AI decisions) is also the person the concern is about. Workers who are worried that the AI adoption is going to be handled badly, or that their role is more at risk than they have been told, need a channel where they can surface that without it immediately becoming a documented conversation with their manager.

This does not require formal whistleblower architecture. It can be as simple as: the external practitioner makes clear that workers can speak with them directly and confidentially about concerns. It provides a pressure valve. Workers who feel they have somewhere to go with a concern they cannot raise directly are more likely to stay engaged with the official process — and less likely to disengage or quietly look for other work.

**Practice 5 — Close the loop.**

Nothing erodes safety faster than a team that has been asked for input and then received nothing in return — no acknowledgment of what they said, no explanation of what was done with it, no visible connection between their input and any decision. This is the standard experience of most organizational surveys and most "listening sessions." The team learns, correctly, that the input was performative.

Close the loop explicitly. "Here is what I heard when I talked to the team. Here is what we are doing differently as a result. Here is what we heard that we are not acting on, and why." This is not complex. It is rare.

**Key takeaway:** Psychological safety is not a culture you install — it is a practice you maintain. Each of these five practices is a behavior, not a value statement.

## Lesson 2B-4: Diagnosing Safety — What to Look For

**Estimated time:** 25 minutes
**Method:** Simulation

---

The practitioner does not wait for a formal survey to assess psychological safety. They read it continuously from the moment they walk in the door.

**Behavioral signals of high safety:**
- Disagreement expressed openly in group settings
- Questions that reveal genuine uncertainty ("I don't understand why we're doing it this way")
- Mistakes acknowledged without elaborate explanation or deflection
- Humor that is inclusive rather than hierarchical
- Junior staff speaking up in the presence of senior staff
- Concerns raised as concerns, not framed as questions to avoid accountability

**Behavioral signals of low safety:**
- Uniform agreement in group settings, especially on topics where disagreement would be natural
- Questions that are really performing compliance ("So we should all be using the new system now, right?")
- Elaborate explanation and qualification around anything that went wrong
- Humor that targets people outside the room, or that positions compliance as virtue
- Only senior people speaking in group settings, or junior people speaking only when directly asked
- Vague, optimistic language about things that are clearly not going well

**What silence means:**

In most organizational contexts, silence on a specific topic is a signal that the topic is not safe. If the team has not asked any questions about what the AI transition means for their jobs — in a context where those questions would be entirely natural — the most likely explanation is not that they do not have the questions. It is that they have learned, from experience, not to ask them. The practitioner does not interpret organizational silence as absence of concern.

**The SMB-specific dynamic:**

In a firm of 10 to 20 people, everyone knows everyone. The informal social fabric is tight. This has two implications for safety. First: low safety is highly contagious. If one person's trust is broken — they raised something and it was met with punishment or dismissal — everyone else witnesses it and updates their behavior accordingly. The sample size is small enough that a single incident shapes the whole team's understanding of what is safe. Second: rebuilding safety after it has been damaged requires the same interpersonal specificity. You cannot send a memo. You have to talk to people, individually, and demonstrate through behavior — over time — that the thing that broke the trust will not happen again.

**Simulation:**

You are observing a team meeting at a 16-person operations firm, called by the owner to introduce a new AI-based project management tool. Here is what you observe:

- The owner speaks for 18 of the 25 minutes. The presentation is polished and confident.
- When he asks "Any questions?", there is a brief silence, then a project coordinator asks a technical question about whether the tool integrates with their existing invoicing software. The owner says he will check.
- Two people are on their phones during the presentation.
- At the end, the owner says "I think we're all excited about where this is going." Three people nod. No one says anything.
- After the meeting, you observe a 90-second conversation in the hallway between two team members. You cannot hear the content, but both are speaking quickly and quietly. They stop when another colleague approaches.

For each observation, identify what it signals about psychological safety and explain your reasoning. Then write a 200-word assessment: what is the safety climate of this team, what is the primary threat to it, and what is the first thing you would address before proceeding with any further discovery work?

**Key takeaway:** What the team does not say is as diagnostic as what they do. Read the silences.

## Lesson 2C-1: The Task Decomposition Methodology

**Estimated time:** 30 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

The starting point for any workforce transition question is not "can we automate this role?" It is "what does this role actually consist of, and which parts of it are automation-amenable?"

These are different questions. The first produces a binary answer that is almost always wrong. The second produces a map that is actually useful.

**The MIT Work of the Future task-level framework:**

The MIT Work of the Future Initiative has produced the most methodologically rigorous task-level analysis of AI displacement in the U.S. workforce. Their approach treats each role not as a unit but as a bundle of tasks, then assesses each task along several dimensions:

*Routinization.* Is this task rule-based and repeatable, or does it require adaptation to novel situations? Routine tasks are more automation-amenable. Non-routine tasks — especially those requiring judgment in novel or ambiguous situations — are less amenable.

*Codifiability.* Can the knowledge required to do this task be written down and transferred? Codifiable knowledge is more automatable. Tacit knowledge — the kind that lives in practice and is built through experience — is much harder to automate reliably.

*Context-dependence.* Does the task require understanding of a specific organizational, relational, or situational context? Context-dependent tasks require exactly the kind of grounded, accumulated understanding that AI models, trained on generic data, do not have.

*Error cost.* What is the consequence of an error in this task? High-error-cost tasks — where an error causes serious harm, triggers liability, or damages a relationship irreversibly — are tasks where human judgment and oversight are typically maintained even when automation is technically possible.

*Relationship-dependence.* Does the task require the trust, responsiveness, or personal knowledge that comes from an ongoing human relationship? Relationship-dependent tasks are among the most automation-resistant because the value is in the relationship, not just the output.

**Applying the framework:**

Take a role and list every recurring task that fills a typical week. Not the job description — the actual work, including the undocumented tasks from the human capacity audit. Then run each task through the five dimensions. Not as a binary automatable/not-automatable classification, but as a spectrum: highly automation-amenable, partially amenable, low amenability, not amenable.

The result is a task map. The automation-amenable tasks show where AI can genuinely add value. The low-amenability tasks — the judgment work, the relationship work, the context-dependent synthesis — show what remains, and what the redesigned role is built around.

**What remains is the point:**

This framework is not primarily a displacement calculator. It is a design tool. The question it answers is not "can we automate this role?" but "if we remove the automation-amenable task substrate from this role, what is left — and is what is left enough to build a meaningful, retained role around?"

In most roles, the answer is yes. The judgment work, the relationship work, the contextual synthesis that accumulates over years — this is substantial, even in roles that appear primarily administrative or routine. The practitioner's job is to find it, name it specifically, and build the redesign around it.

In some roles, honestly assessed, the answer is not clearly yes. That is the hard case addressed in Lesson 2C-5.

**Key takeaway:** Task decomposition is not about deciding who to eliminate. It is about understanding what each person actually does, so that the redesign is built on reality rather than a job description.

## Lesson 2C-2: Strengths Anchoring and Internal Mobility

**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Method:** Reading + simulation

---

Once you know what remains in a role after task decomposition, the next question is: does what remains align with what this person does well? And if not — is there somewhere else in the organization where it would?

**Strengths anchoring:**

The task decomposition gives you the map of what the redesigned role contains. The strengths-and-crafting work from Module 2A gives you the map of what this person contributes at their best. Strengths anchoring is the process of aligning the two: building the redesigned role around the tasks that remain and that this person's strengths position them to do exceptionally well.

This is not always possible within the original role. Sometimes what remains in a role — after removing the automation-amenable substrate — does not map to this person's strengths. The client relationship work that survives may require a relational intensity that this person finds draining. The judgment synthesis that survives may require comfort with ambiguity that this person does not have. When this happens, the redesign needs to look beyond the original role.

**Internal mobility architecture:**

Before concluding that there is no role for a person, the practitioner looks across the whole organization. The question is not "is there a redesigned version of this person's current role?" but "is there anywhere in this organization where this person's human capacity — their specific strengths, their institutional knowledge, their relationships — would be genuinely valuable?"

This requires understanding the organization well enough to see possibilities that the organization itself may not have considered. The operations coordinator whose role is largely automated may have the relationship intelligence to move to a client-facing role. The administrative manager whose scheduling and data-entry tasks are absorbed may have the organizational memory to become an internal knowledge management and training resource. The match is not always obvious from a job title view. It often is obvious from a human capacity view.

The practice prompt for the practitioner: before any workforce transition conversation with a client, map the internal mobility options explicitly. Present them as genuine options, not as performance. If there is a plausible redesign or redeployment, name it specifically and advocate for it.

**The AT&T case — what full-effort transition looks like at scale:**

AT&T's Future Ready initiative, launched in 2013 and extended through the decade, committed over $1 billion to retraining a workforce facing rapid technical displacement. The specific elements: transparent multi-year planning communicated directly to affected workers, internal mobility pathways including lateral moves to roles with longer horizon, voluntary separation packages for workers who chose not to reskill, and active investment in career development for those who stayed.

The results were mixed. Some of the retraining investment did not produce the role transitions that were intended. Some workers were displaced despite the program. But the Sucher research on what happened to trust during and after the transition is instructive: workers who experienced the transition as genuinely effortful — who felt the organization had tried — reported substantially better outcomes in terms of trust, subsequent engagement, and the quality of their departure (for those who did leave) than workers who experienced the transition as pretextual.

The SMB equivalent is not $1 billion. It is honest internal review, genuine role redesign, real rather than performative consideration of mobility options, and — if departure is ultimately required — a transition handled with the dignity the person's contribution has earned.

**Simulation:**

A 16-person financial advisory firm is implementing an AI tool that will handle initial client portfolio analysis, regulatory reporting preparation, and meeting scheduling — tasks currently distributed across three roles: two Financial Analysts and an Administrative Coordinator.

You have completed the human capacity audit for all three. Here is what you know:

- Financial Analyst 1 (Maya, 7 years): Her automation-amenable tasks are portfolio analysis templates and regulatory reporting prep. What remains: client relationship management for 14 long-term clients, complex scenario planning for unusual situations, new client onboarding, and mentoring the junior analyst. Her strengths-and-crafting profile: high relational intelligence, energized by complex problem-solving, wants more strategic involvement with clients.

- Financial Analyst 2 (James, 2 years): His automation-amenable tasks are the same analysis and reporting templates. What remains: initial portfolio analysis for newer clients, regulatory reporting review (the AI does the first draft, someone must review), and research synthesis. His strengths-and-crafting profile: strong analytical detail orientation, finds client relationship management draining, interested in a deeper technical or research path.

- Administrative Coordinator (Priya, 4 years): Her automation-amenable tasks are scheduling and initial data entry. What remains: coordination of complex multi-party situations, client onboarding logistics (the human judgment parts), internal communications, and vendor management. Her institutional memory profile: knows every client's communication preferences, handles every exception case, the person the rest of the team goes to when something unusual happens.

For each person: conduct the task decomposition assessment, propose a redesigned role, and identify the internal mobility considerations. Then write a 200-word summary of what you would say to the owner about the workforce planning decision overall.

**Key takeaway:** Internal mobility is not a consolation prize. It is a genuine design question about where each person's human capacity creates the most value in the organization.

## Lesson 2C-3: The Responsible-Restructuring Evidence

**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Method:** Reading + case analysis

---

Every practitioner who does workforce transition work needs to know what the research says about the consequences of workforce reductions — not to prevent clients from making difficult decisions, but to ensure those decisions are made with full information about what they cost.

**The Sucher findings:**

Sandra Sucher's research on trust and workforce reduction, synthesized in *The Power of Trust*, documents what happens to organizational trust when workforce reductions occur. The findings are not what most leaders expect. Layoffs — even when the business rationale is defensible — damage trust not just with the people who leave, but with the people who stay, with customers, and with the broader community.

The mechanism is consistent: the implicit contract of the employment relationship (the organization will take care of its people when it can) is violated, and everyone who observes the violation updates their model of what the organization is actually committed to. Customers who depend on relationship continuity worry about their own stability. Suppliers recalibrate their partnership assumptions. Surviving employees — regardless of whether they personally feel secure — recalibrate their assessment of how secure they actually are.

The trust damage from a workforce reduction can last years. The productivity improvements that were supposed to follow often do not materialize at the projected rate, because the survivors are carrying the psychological weight of the reduction and often doing more work with less support.

**The Cascio findings:**

Wayne Cascio's responsible restructuring research reaches a striking conclusion: only about 30% of layoffs improve profit margins three or more years out. The short-term cost savings are real. The long-term performance improvement is, in the majority of cases, not.

Cascio's work identifies the organizations that do outperform after restructuring: they are the ones that engaged in genuine alternatives before layoffs — redeployment, voluntary reduced hours, voluntary separation packages, retraining investments — and that treated the reduction as a last resort rather than a first response. The organizations that defaulted to involuntary separation as the primary tool consistently underperformed the alternatives on long-term financial metrics.

For the practitioner, this is not a moral argument. It is a business case. Presenting the responsible-restructuring evidence to a client before they make a workforce reduction decision is not a lecture — it is providing the information a competent advisor is obligated to provide before a consequential decision.

**Brockner and survivor syndrome:**

Joel Brockner's research on surviving employees — those who remain after a layoff event — documents what is now called survivor syndrome: a predictable pattern of reduced commitment, increased turnover intention, and diminished performance among workers who kept their jobs after others did not. The magnitude of these effects depends on several factors: how the reduction was handled, whether the survivors perceived it as fair, whether they have ongoing uncertainty about future reductions, and whether they had relationships with the people who left.

The survivor syndrome finding is practically important for the SMB context. In a 20-person firm, there is no reduction that is not visible and personal. Everyone knows the people who left. Everyone is recalibrating their understanding of what the firm is. A reduction that is handled carelessly — even if the business decision was sound — can produce survivor effects that cost more, over the following 12–18 months, than the reduction saved.

**The Klarna teaching case:**

In 2023 and 2024, Klarna publicly celebrated its AI-driven workforce reduction, attributing significant headcount decreases to AI capability. The narrative was widely covered: AI is doing the work of hundreds of customer service employees.

In 2025, the picture was more complicated. Klarna began rehiring customer service workers. The quality of AI-handled customer interactions had not matched the quality of human-handled interactions at the level initially projected. The tacit knowledge that the customer service team had carried — about how to handle complex, emotional, non-standard situations — turned out to be more difficult to replicate than the initial productivity measurements suggested.

The case does not mean AI customer service does not work. It means that the task decomposition for customer service work had underestimated the judgment-intensive and relationship-intensive components — exactly the category of work that the human capacity audit is designed to surface. The lesson for the practitioner: the automation-amenable assessment needs to be honest, and the human capacity inventory needs to be thorough, before a workforce reduction decision is made. Klarna's reversal was expensive and reputationally complicated. It is the cost of incomplete assessment.

**Key takeaway:** The responsible-restructuring evidence is not a moral argument against reduction — it is information a client needs before making a decision. Providing it is part of the practitioner's job.

## Lesson 2C-4: The Non-Replacement Pattern

**Estimated time:** 20 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

The most common 2025–2026 workforce reduction pattern at SMB scale is not a layoff announcement. It is the quiet decision not to backfill when someone leaves.

A person resigns. The owner decides not to hire a replacement — the AI tools can handle most of what they did, or the remaining team can absorb the rest. This happens once, then again. Headcount declines not through elimination but through attrition, and each attrition is handled as a one-time operational decision rather than a workforce planning decision.

The organizational consequences of this pattern are distinctive and often invisible until they are significant.

**What the non-replacement pattern costs:**

*Skill loss without acknowledgment.* When a person leaves and their role is not backfilled, whatever they carried — the institutional memory, the tacit knowledge, the relationships — leaves with them. Unlike a layoff, where there is at least the possibility of a transition period, a voluntary departure followed by non-replacement often means the knowledge is gone before the organization has registered that it mattered.

*Work absorption without reconfiguration.* The work the person was doing does not disappear. It gets absorbed — formally or informally — by the people who remain. If that absorption is not deliberately managed, the remaining staff are now doing more work without additional compensation, support, or acknowledgment. The engagement decline that follows is predictable.

*Cumulative fragility.* A single non-replacement may be entirely defensible. A pattern of non-replacements produces cumulative fragility: the organization is getting smaller while its commitments have not shrunk proportionally, and the people who remain are carrying an increasing load. The organization that looked like it was running efficiently is, on inspection, running on the goodwill and overtime of a shrinking team.

**How the practitioner identifies it:**

The signals are observable through the human capacity audit and the people interviews. Employees who describe doing work that "used to be someone else's job" without any formal role change. Owners who reference "natural attrition" with an optimism that does not match the underlying situation. Org charts that do not reflect the actual current headcount because updating them was lower priority than everything else. Job descriptions that describe a role that has, in practice, been absorbed by two or three other people.

**How the practitioner names it:**

Directly, and without accusation. "I want to name something I've observed, because I think it's creating a risk that we should address as part of this engagement. It looks like there have been several departures over the last 18 months where the role wasn't backfilled, and the work has been absorbed by the remaining team. That may be the right decision in each individual case — but as a pattern, it's producing an increasing workload on the people you have, and it means you may be losing institutional knowledge faster than you're accounting for. That's worth looking at directly."

**Key takeaway:** The non-replacement pattern is a workforce reduction that does not announce itself as one. The practitioner names it for what it is.

## Lesson 2C-5: The Hard Case

**Estimated time:** 30 minutes
**Method:** Reading + reflection

---

Everything in this domain up to this point is about finding the path to human continuity — to role redesign, to internal mobility, to the elevation of human contribution as AI absorbs the task substrate. That work is real, and it is worth doing thoroughly.

And sometimes, after it has been done thoroughly and in good faith, the answer is: there is no role here. The task decomposition leaves a remainder that is not enough to build a retained role around. The strengths anchoring finds no match within the current or plausible future organizational structure. The internal mobility review finds no position where this person's human capacity creates value that justifies retention.

This is the hard case. The practitioner must be able to navigate it.

**First: be certain it is genuinely a hard case.**

The most common error is calling it a hard case prematurely. A human capacity audit done in a 30-minute conversation is not a hard case determination. A task decomposition done from a job description without direct observation is not a hard case determination. A role redesign discussion that has not genuinely explored internal mobility is not a hard case determination.

The hard case determination requires: a thorough human capacity inventory conducted through direct conversation and observation; a complete task decomposition done with the actual work, not the documented version; a genuine internal mobility review that looks across the organization; and an honest assessment of whether any of the redesign possibilities are organizationally feasible within a realistic timeframe.

If that work has been done and the answer is still no — then it is the hard case.

**What the practitioner does in the hard case:**

Name it clearly to the client. Not softened, not buried in a recommendation report. "We've done the full assessment. Here is what this person contributes — [specifics]. Here is what we explored in terms of redesign and redeployment — [specifics]. Here is why we believe a retained role is not achievable in the current context. This is the decision you are facing."

Ensure the departure is handled with dignity. The person's contribution is named and acknowledged — not managed as a risk event. What is happening and why is communicated honestly, not dressed as a restructuring when it is an elimination. Transition support, adequate notice, and honest references are the minimum. The practitioner advocates for more where it is possible.

Document what is being lost. What institutional knowledge leaves with this person? What relationships? What judgment? This is not sentiment — it is organizational intelligence. Ensure the client understands specifically what they are losing, because that understanding is what motivates the knowledge capture and succession planning that should accompany the departure.

**What the practitioner does not do:**

They do not write the communication that misrepresents an elimination as a restructuring. They do not provide language that obscures what is actually happening. They do not allow their deliverable to be used as cover for a decision that was made before the assessment was done.

If a client has already made the decision before the practitioner completes the assessment, that is a different situation — and requires a direct conversation about what the practitioner can and cannot do. "I can help you think through the transition. I can't help you communicate it as something it isn't."

**Reflection:**

Think about a workforce reduction you have witnessed or been part of — in any context. What was communicated about why it was happening? What was actually true? What was the gap between those two things, and what did that gap cost — in trust, in morale, in your own relationship with the organization?

Write 200 words on what the practitioner's obligation is when they know the gap exists and the client is planning to communicate something that is not fully accurate.

**Key takeaway:** The hard case is where the practitioner's values are tested. Navigating it with integrity does not mean stopping the decision — it means ensuring it is made with full information, handled with dignity, and communicated honestly.

---

**Discussion prompt:** A client says to you, "We've decided to let two people go as part of this AI rollout. Can you help us communicate it?" What do you need to know before you can respond to that request? What would you do if, on investigation, you believed the decision was premature or avoidable?

**AI assistant prompt:** Ask the curriculum assistant: "What does the research on layoff survivor syndrome actually show about the magnitude and duration of effects — and are there documented interventions that reduce survivor syndrome after a workforce reduction?"

## Lesson 2D-1: Integrating the Three Components

**Estimated time:** 30 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

The three modules that precede this one each build a distinct capability: the human capacity audit (what each person actually contributes), the psychological safety assessment (the conditions that determine whether the audit can happen honestly), and the workforce transition framework (what to do when that contribution is at risk from AI displacement). Module 2D is where those three capabilities become a single coherent practice.

**The sequencing in the field:**

In a real engagement, the three components do not happen in neat sequential phases. They are concurrent and mutually informing.

The safety assessment begins before the first formal conversation. You are reading the safety climate from the moment you walk in — the tone of the space, the energy in the room, who talks to whom, what gets said in your presence and what gets said when you are not quite in earshot. By the time you are in a first formal conversation with the owner, you already have a working hypothesis about the safety climate.

The human capacity audit runs through every conversation — with the owner, with managers, with front-line staff. Every conversation surfaces pieces of the picture: who is mentioned as essential, who is not mentioned, what knowledge seems to live in one place, what processes appear to depend on one person. You are building the capacity map continuously, from multiple perspectives.

The workforce transition flags appear early. The owner mentions something about "streamlining the team." A manager alludes to a conversation that was had about "what the AI means for the department." Someone in a front-line conversation says something that reveals they are uncertain about their own position. These are flags, not findings — they require investigation before they become conclusions.

**The integration principle:**

The people audit is not three separate instruments applied sequentially. It is a single sustained practice of observation, conversation, and synthesis, organized around three questions:

- What does each person actually contribute? (capacity)
- What conditions determine whether this team will engage honestly with what we are doing here? (safety)
- Where are the workforce transition implications — and have they been thought through? (transition)

The practitioner holds all three questions simultaneously throughout the first phase of the engagement.

**Key takeaway:** The people audit is a practice, not a process. The three components are lenses, not sequential steps.

## Lesson 2D-2: Triangulating Across Levels

**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Method:** Reading + simulation

---

In any organization of more than a few people, information is stratified by level. What the owner knows, believes, and chooses to say is different from what the manager knows and says, which is different from what the front-line staff knows and says. This is not simply a question of access to information — it is also a question of incentive, role, and safety.

The gaps between what is said at different levels are where the real information is.

**What each level typically knows and does not know:**

*The owner / principal:* Knows the strategic picture, the financial situation, the decisions that are being considered. Often does not know the day-to-day operational reality — what is actually breaking, which processes require workarounds, which staff relationships are under stress. Often has a more optimistic view of team morale and communication than the reality warrants. Often knows about workforce planning that has not been communicated to the team.

*Managers and team leads:* Know the operational reality more precisely than the owner. Know where the processes break and who carries the workarounds. Often caught in the middle — knowing things that the front-line staff do not know and things that they have been asked not to share. Often the people who bear the most cost when communication between ownership and staff is poor, because they are asked to absorb and contain the uncertainty.

*Front-line staff:* Know the work with the highest fidelity. Know which parts of the process are more complex than the documentation suggests. Know which tools work and which do not in daily practice. Often have accurate intuitions about organizational health and the workforce implications of changes — more accurate than they are credited for. Often the most honest about what is not working, if they feel safe enough to say it.

**How to triangulate:**

Ask the same question, in different forms appropriate to each level. "How is the team responding to the AI transition?" asked of the owner, the manager, and a front-line staff member will produce three answers. The overlap is what is shared; the gaps are what each level is filtering. The practitioner is interested in both.

Name the gaps directly — internally, for your own synthesis, and eventually in the people-layer summary. "The owner describes the team as broadly engaged with the transition. The front-line staff, in individual conversations, described significant unspoken anxiety about role implications. The manager was aware of the anxiety and had not surfaced it upward." This is the real picture. It is more useful than any single account.

**Reading the structural signals:**

Beyond the content of what is said, the structure of who says what reveals the information architecture of the organization. If every significant piece of information comes through the owner — if staff conversations circle back to "you should talk to [owner]" — that is a founder bottleneck, which is also a process risk (addressed in Domain 3). If staff consistently contradict each other about what the current policy or process is, that is a governance problem. If managers speak primarily in terms of what ownership wants rather than what they observe, the middle layer has been functionally disintermediated.

**Simulation:**

You are doing your first round of conversations at a 17-person marketing firm. Here is what you heard at each level:

*Owner (Sophie):* "The team is really excited about the new tools. We've had a couple of team meetings where everyone was really engaged. The creative staff especially — they see how this frees them up to do more interesting work. One person, Marcus, has been resistant — he's been with us 11 years and struggles with change — but I think he'll come around."

*Creative Director (team manager, Jess):* "The tools are interesting. There's a learning curve. A few people are worried about what it means long-term — I've tried to reassure them, but I'm not sure I've succeeded. Sophie's been very positive about it in the team meetings, which is great. Marcus has had some real concerns that I think are worth hearing — he knows more about how we actually produce work than the description suggests."

*Front-line staff (two brief conversations):* Creative (Rina, 3 years): "It's fine. The tools are interesting. I just want to know what the plan is — nobody's really said what this means for the team." Production coordinator (Marcus, 11 years): "I have concerns. I've raised them. I'm not sure anyone is really listening. What I do is hard to describe, and I worry people are underestimating what would be missing if it were automated."

Conduct the triangulation analysis. What is consistent across levels? Where are the gaps, and what do the gaps suggest? What is the most important thing you would investigate next, and how would you do it?

*Debrief:* Marcus is the practitioner's most important conversation in this engagement. Not because he is resistant — but because the gap between how the owner describes him ("resistant, struggles with change") and how the manager describes him ("real concerns, knows more than the description suggests") and how he describes himself ("concerns raised, not heard") is exactly the signal that his human capacity is both significant and under-recognized. The 11-year tenure alone is a flag: that is 11 years of institutional knowledge, and the owner is framing it primarily as a change-management problem.

**Key takeaway:** Triangulate before concluding. One account is a perspective. Three accounts from different levels, compared carefully, are the beginning of a picture.

## Lesson 2D-3: Reading What Is Not Said

**Estimated time:** 25 minutes
**Method:** Reading

---

The practitioner's most important skill is not asking good questions. It is noticing what is not said in response to the questions that were asked.

**The topics people avoid:**

Every organization has topics that have become, through experience, not-safe-to-name. In AI-era SMBs, these topics cluster predictably: job security, the gap between what the AI was supposed to do and what it actually does, whose decision it was to adopt this tool and whether that decision was a good one, who is carrying the most burden from the transition, and what the actual plan is for the workforce. If any of these topics produces deflection, vagueness, or a redirect to a different subject, that is a signal. The deflection is information.

**The questions that produce deflection:**

Watch for the specific pattern: a direct question produces an answer that addresses a nearby topic rather than the question asked. "What do you think this transition means for the team long-term?" answered with "The tools are really exciting, there's a lot of potential" is a deflection. It is not dishonesty — it may be genuine optimism, or it may be an answer to the safer version of the question. Either way, the fact that the actual question was not addressed is worth noting.

**Body language and behavioral signals:**

The practitioner is not a trained behavioral analyst and should not pretend to be. But basic observational attention is both appropriate and useful: the speed with which someone changes a subject, the quality of eye contact when a topic is named, the shift in posture or energy when a question touches something sensitive. These signals do not produce certainty — they produce hypotheses that are worth testing through follow-up questions or through triangulation with other conversations.

**The things people say sideways:**

In low-safety environments, people often communicate real concerns obliquely. "I'm sure it'll all work out" said without conviction. "I trust that leadership knows what they're doing" said in a way that makes it unclear whether it is sincere. The self-deprecating comment about their own role that contains, embedded in the humor, a real fear. "I'm probably not the best person to ask about the AI stuff" said by the person with 15 years of relevant experience.

The practitioner notes these and returns to them. "You said you're probably not the best person to ask — but you've been here a long time and you clearly know how this actually works. What would you say if you were the best person to ask?"

**Key takeaway:** The silence is part of the data. The practitioner reads the whole conversation — including the parts that are not spoken.

## Lesson 2D-4: Producing the People-Layer Summary

**Estimated time:** 30 minutes
**Method:** Reading + simulation

---

The people-layer summary is the output of the first-phase engagement work. It is a written document that captures what the practitioner has observed about the human terrain of the organization — and that informs the subsequent process and technology audit without reducing the people to data points.

**What the summary contains:**

*Human capacity overview.* For the organization as a whole: where are the significant concentrations of tacit knowledge? Who carries institutional memory that is not documented anywhere? Where are the key client and vendor relationships that are personal rather than institutional? Where are the workforce transition risks?

*Individual capacity profiles (key people only).* For the people where the capacity picture is most important to the subsequent work — typically the people most affected by the AI transition and the people most critical to the process audit — a brief profile: what they actually contribute, what the redesign direction is, any workforce transition flags.

*Safety climate assessment.* Overall safety climate, the primary factors driving it, any safety paradox observations, and the first-priority interventions before the engagement proceeds.

*Workforce transition flags.* Any situations requiring the responsible-restructuring framework, any hard case indicators that need further assessment, any non-replacement patterns that should be named.

*What the people-layer tells us about the process and technology audit.* This is the bridge to Domains 3 and 4. Where do processes live primarily in people rather than documentation? Where does the technology audit need to account for human capacity that AI adoption might affect? What does the safety climate tell us about the organization's readiness for the changes we may be about to recommend?

**What the summary does not contain:**

Performance assessments. The people-layer summary is not a performance review of the organization's staff. It says what people contribute and what the organizational conditions are — not whether people are doing their jobs correctly.

Recommendations before the diagnosis is complete. The process and technology audits have not happened yet. The people-layer summary informs those audits — it does not pre-empt their findings with workforce recommendations.

More detail than the subsequent audit needs. The summary is a working document, not a final report. It should be precise, not comprehensive. If a person's capacity profile is not relevant to the subsequent work, it does not need to be in the summary.

**Simulation:**

Write a people-layer summary for the 17-person marketing firm from Lesson 2D-2. Based on the triangulation work you did, produce: the human capacity overview, individual profiles for Sophie (owner), Jess (creative director), and Marcus (production coordinator), the safety climate assessment, the workforce transition flags, and a brief bridge section identifying what the people-layer tells you about the process and technology audit to come.

Limit: 500 words. Every word should earn its place.

**Key takeaway:** The people-layer summary is a working document, not a final report. Its job is to ensure that the process and technology audits are built on an accurate understanding of the human terrain — so that the recommendations that eventually come out of the engagement are ones that the people inside the organization can actually use.

---

**Discussion prompt:** The process audit in Domain 3 will reveal operational problems that often turn out to be people problems — workflows that depend on tacit knowledge no one has documented, processes that only function because of specific relationships. How does the people-layer summary prepare you to see that correctly?

**Module 2D Assessment**

**Type:** Integrated people-layer summary

**Prompt:** You are given a composite case: a first-contact brief, notes from four conversations (owner, operations manager, two front-line staff members), and three observations from your time on-site at a fictional 21-person logistics firm.

*(a)* Conduct the triangulation analysis: what is consistent across accounts, where are the gaps, and what do the gaps suggest?

*(b)* Identify every safety signal in the case materials — positive and negative — and produce a safety climate assessment.

*(c)* Identify the workforce transition flags present in the case.

*(d)* Write the people-layer summary (400 words maximum) that you would bring into the process audit.

**What it measures:** Triangulation skill; safety signal recognition; workforce transition flag identification; integration of the three Domain 2 components into a coherent summary; disciplined synthesis that identifies what matters without reducing people to data points.

## Domain 2 Capstone Reflection

**Prompt:** Write 300–500 words responding to the following situation:

You have completed the people audit at a 19-person operations firm. Your findings include: two clear hard case indicators (people whose roles appear unlikely to survive the AI transition in their current form), a safety paradox (the owner is asking for honest engagement feedback while running undisclosed workforce planning), and a non-replacement pattern that has reduced headcount from 24 to 19 over 18 months without any formal acknowledgment.

The owner wants to move quickly to the process and technology audit. You have not yet addressed any of the people-layer findings with them.
