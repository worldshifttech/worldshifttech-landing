## Domain 1 Overview

**Total estimated engagement time:** 5–6 hours across all four modules
**Prerequisites:** None — this is the curriculum entry point

Domain 1 does three things. It grounds you in what is measurably true about AI adoption and work in 2026. It trains you to distinguish data from projection and projection from hype. And it builds the internal orientation — the *stance* — that everything else in this curriculum assumes.

A practitioner who skips this domain will misread clients. They will mistake anxiety for resistance. They will recommend change to teams that are not safe enough to receive it. They will cite vendor statistics that collapse under scrutiny. Domain 1 prevents all of that.

**The four modules:**
- **1A** — What is actually happening: the data layer
- **1B** — Who is thriving, who is struggling, and why
- **1C** — The worker's experience
- **1D** — Reading projections honestly

# MODULE 1A — What Is Actually Happening: The Data Layer

**Estimated engagement time:** 1.5 hours

**Learning objectives:** By the end of this module, you can:

1. Cite the actual independent data on AI adoption in SMBs — U.S. Census BTOS figures, Stanford Digital Economy Lab findings, MIT NANDA results — without relying on vendor or media sources.
2. Explain the difference between shadow adoption and strategic adoption, and identify which mode most SMBs are actually in.
3. Articulate the productivity paradox: why AI reliably raises task-level productivity in controlled settings but unreliably raises firm-level productivity in real SMB conditions, and why the gap is organizational rather than technical.
4. Distinguish a measured finding from a projected finding in the AI-and-work literature.

## Lesson 1A-1: The Actual Adoption Picture

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 30 minutes

---

### The number you need to know first

Here is the number the AI industry does not lead with: as of late 2025, approximately 7–9% of U.S. businesses report using AI for the production of goods or services.

That figure comes from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS), which since 2023 has surveyed a rotating sample of roughly 1.2 million U.S. firms. It is the largest, most methodologically rigorous ongoing dataset on actual business AI use in existence. It is not a vendor study. It is not a survey of firms self-selected because they are interested in AI. It is a randomized, representative sample of American business, asking a simple operational question: is your business using AI to produce what you sell?

For small firms — under 50 employees — the number is lower than the overall figure. The gap between firms with fewer than 20 employees and firms with more than 250 is roughly 3x. A small business is meaningfully less likely to be using AI in its operations than a larger one, and the gap is not closing quickly.

This matters because you will enter client conversations with people who have absorbed a very different picture. They have read vendor whitepapers, LinkedIn posts, and business media that consistently report AI adoption in the range of 40–75%, often citing "use at any level" or "employees who have tried AI tools." Those figures are not wrong — they just measure something different. Reported AI use by any employee inside an SMB, including informal personal use of ChatGPT, Claude, or Copilot, is indeed far higher. But informal personal use is not the same thing as the firm using AI to produce what it makes and sells. That distinction is the first thing you need to be able to hold clearly, and to explain clearly.

### Where adoption actually concentrates

The BTOS figures are not uniformly distributed across the business population. Independent analysis of the data (Bonney, Breaux, Buffington, Dinlersoz, Foster et al., U.S. Census Center for Economic Studies, 2024–2025) found that strategic AI use clusters in three places: information sector firms, firms with existing data infrastructure, and firms in metro areas. The concentration is steep.

What this means in practice: AI adoption is not simply a function of how forward-thinking an owner is. It correlates with whether the firm already had structured data — a real CRM, documented workflows, clean financial reporting. Firms without that infrastructure have significantly less to layer AI onto, and attempts to add AI on top of unstructured operations consistently underperform. You will see this pattern repeatedly in the field. The business that wants to "use AI to organize things" has it backwards: the organizing has to happen first.

### The Stanford signal: something real is happening at the career-entry level

Separate from the BTOS adoption data, the Stanford Digital Economy Lab published a 2025 working paper (Brynjolfsson, Chandar et al.) using ADP payroll microdata — not surveys, actual employment records from one of the largest payroll processors in the country. The finding: a measurable decline in entry-level employment in AI-exposed occupations (software development, customer support, certain administrative roles) for workers aged 22–25, while older workers in the same occupations remained stable or grew slightly.

This is not the dramatic collapse of whole job categories that many projections describe. It is something more specific: AI appears to be compressing the bottom of certain career ladders. Junior positions that historically served as entry points into knowledge-work careers are disappearing or not being posted, while the senior roles those junior positions would have fed into remain intact or are growing.

That is a different kind of disruption than the headline version, and it has particular relevance for the people layer of your work — which Domain 2 covers. For now, hold the finding as suggestive rather than settled. It has not been fully replicated, and a single working paper using any methodology, however strong, is not a conclusion. What it does give you is a real empirical signal — and a more precise, accurate thing to say when clients ask whether AI is taking jobs.

### What the data actually says versus what you will be told it says

A useful habit for everything in Domain 1: distinguish the source before accepting the figure.

| Claim | Source | What it actually measures |
|---|---|---|
| "40–75% of employees use AI" | Industry surveys, vendor studies | Self-reported personal use by any employee |
| "7–9% of businesses use AI" | U.S. Census BTOS | Firms using AI in production, operational definition |
| "AI will create 170M jobs, displace 92M" | WEF Future of Jobs 2025 | Employer survey projections — not observations |
| "Entry-level tech employment declining" | Stanford / ADP payroll microdata | Actual employment records, AI-exposed occupations |
| "95% of AI pilots produce no P&L impact" | MIT NANDA, 2025 | ~300 enterprise deployments studied directly |

The last row in that table tends to surprise people the most, and it is the subject of Lesson 1A-3. Before getting there, Lesson 1A-2 covers what is actually happening inside most small businesses — which is neither nothing nor strategy.

---

**Key takeaway:** The real AI adoption rate in SMBs is far lower than vendor-influenced media suggests. Independent Census data tells a sober, concentrated story: adoption clusters in specific sectors and firm types, entry-level labor market effects are real but narrow, and most small businesses are not yet operating with AI as a deliberate business tool.

## Lesson 1A-2: Shadow Adoption — The Hidden Signal

**Type:** Reading + Reflection | **Estimated time:** 25 minutes

---

### Two kinds of AI use

When the BTOS reports that roughly 7–9% of U.S. businesses are using AI in production, and when industry surveys simultaneously report that 40–75% of employees are using AI tools, both numbers can be true at once. They are measuring different things.

The gap between them has a name: shadow adoption.

Shadow adoption is individual AI use — employees using ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, or any of the proliferating AI-augmented SaaS tools in their day-to-day work — that happens without the firm having made any deliberate decisions about it. No policy. No governance. No designated workflow. The employee found a tool, it made something easier, and they kept using it. Management may not know. Other employees may not know. The client receiving the AI-assisted deliverable almost certainly does not know.

This is distinct from strategic adoption: the firm has thought about which work should involve AI, who is responsible for those decisions, what the governance looks like, how AI output is reviewed, and what the implications are for roles and workflows. Strategic adoption involves decisions made at the firm level, not by individuals acting alone.

The single most important field condition to internalize before your first client engagement: **most SMBs do not have an AI strategy. They have AI behavior.**

The Microsoft/LinkedIn Work Trend Index data from 2024–2025, which tracks AI tool use patterns across millions of workers, found that the majority of employees who use AI at work are doing so with tools they brought themselves — not tools their employer chose, provisioned, or has visibility into. Microsoft coined the term BYOAI (bring-your-own-AI) for this pattern. It is the dominant mode.

### Why this matters for your work

Shadow adoption is not a character problem. It is not a compliance problem. It is an organizational problem with a consistent set of causes.

**Workflow pain.** People use AI tools in the shadow because a real workflow problem is making their work harder, and they found something that helps. The specific tasks people shadow-adopt AI for tend to be the same across organizations: first-draft writing, summarization, formatting, research synthesis, repetitive data manipulation. These are tasks where the productivity benefit is immediate, personal, and obvious — and where the organizational implications (data security, output quality, confidentiality) are invisible from where the individual sits.

**Governance vacuum.** Most SMBs have no AI policy. They often have no explicit conversation with staff about AI. In the absence of guidance, people make reasonable individual choices that may be poor collective ones. Someone shares client data with a free AI tool to generate a proposal. Someone uses AI to draft a legal communication that goes out without review. Someone's role is quietly shifting toward AI-assisted output, and neither they nor their manager has named what is changing.

**Signal about opportunity.** The tasks where shadow adoption concentrates are, almost by definition, the tasks where AI could add the most value if used deliberately. Employees found the real productivity gains before the organization did. Shadow adoption is a map.

### The reframe that matters

Most SMB owners, when they discover shadow AI use, have one of two reactions: dismissal ("it's just ChatGPT, it doesn't matter") or alarm ("this is a data security problem"). Both reactions miss the more useful interpretation.

Shadow adoption is a diagnostic signal. It tells you where the real workflow friction is, where AI actually adds value at the individual level in this specific business, where governance gaps exist as design opportunities, and which employees are already comfortable with AI tools and might be first adopters for something more deliberate.

When you enter a client's organization and start discovery, you will ask about AI use. People will tell you less than is actually happening — not because they are dishonest, but because they are uncertain what you want to hear, uncertain whether they are doing something wrong, and uncertain whether their AI use is even worth mentioning. Lesson 1C-1 covers the psychological dynamics in full. For now: assume shadow adoption is present and significant in every client firm you work with, even those whose leadership says "we're not really using AI yet."

### Reflection prompt

*Take 5 minutes to write a response before reading on.*

Think about a business you know well — your own, a past employer, a client's. Where, specifically, do you think shadow AI use is most likely happening? What workflow pain would be driving it? What governance gap does it reveal?

---

**Key takeaway:** Most SMBs are not in an AI strategy — they are in AI behavior, driven by individual employees acting without organizational guidance. Shadow adoption is not a violation to police; it is a diagnostic signal about where the real work is.

## Lesson 1A-3: The Productivity Paradox

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 25 minutes

---

### The finding that got reported wrong

In July 2025, MIT's NANDA initiative published "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" (Challapally, Zhao et al.). The study examined approximately 300 AI deployments across enterprise organizations and concluded that roughly 95% of generative AI pilots produced no measurable profit-and-loss impact.

This was widely reported as "95% of AI projects fail." That framing is wrong, and it matters that it is wrong, because the actual finding is more useful — and more directly relevant to your consulting practice — than the simplified version.

The NANDA study's argument is not that AI doesn't work. It is that most pilots fail to cross a specific gap: from working demo to integrated workflow. The failure mode is not technical. The AI tools perform as advertised. The failure is organizational: organizations do not redesign the work around the tool, do not invest in the change management required for consistent adoption, do not establish governance, and do not train people to use AI in ways that produce reliable output. The pilot demonstrates possibility; the organization lacks the infrastructure to convert possibility into sustained, measurable business impact.

Independent corroboration has come from multiple sources. Gartner reported that more than 40% of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by end of 2027 due to cost, value, and risk issues. BCG's own client research tracks consistently with the NANDA finding at the firm-productivity level.

### Where AI does reliably work: the controlled study evidence

Separate from the deployment studies, there is a body of well-controlled experimental research on individual task productivity. This evidence is consistent and compelling.

**Writing.** Noy and Zhang (2023, *Science*) ran a randomized controlled trial in which workers given access to GPT-4 for writing tasks completed those tasks 40% faster with equivalent or higher quality ratings than control-group workers. Clean experimental design in a controlled setting.

**Customer service.** Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025) studied 5,000+ customer service agents over 14 months in a live deployment and found that agents using AI assistance handled 14% more conversations per hour with improved customer satisfaction. Notably, the largest gains accrued to the *least experienced* workers — AI gave junior agents access to the institutional knowledge senior agents had accumulated over years.

**Software development.** GitHub Copilot studies (Peng et al. 2023 and multiple follow-ons) consistently find that developers using AI code completion finish tasks 30–55% faster than those without it, with the range depending heavily on task type and developer experience.

**Consulting tasks.** Dell'Acqua et al. (Harvard Business School, 2023 — the "Cyborgs and Centaurs" study, still the most carefully designed study in this space) found that BCG consultants using GPT-4 on structured consulting tasks performed 23% better on quality scores and 25% faster than those without access. The same study found, however, that consultants using AI *outside its competence* — on tasks where AI was unreliable — performed *worse* than the no-AI control group. They over-trusted the output and failed to apply critical judgment. This second finding is as important as the first.

### Why individual gains don't add up to firm gains

The research creates a genuine puzzle: task-level productivity gains of 14–55% are large and consistent. Why, then, do 95% of enterprise AI deployments produce no measurable P&L impact?

The answer is organizational, and it points directly at what this consulting practice exists to address.

**The gap is not caught by the pilot.** A pilot measures whether a tool works for a specific task in a controlled setting. It does not measure whether the firm can absorb the change — redesign the workflow, shift roles, establish quality control, manage the anxiety and confusion that come with any significant operational change. Pilots skip the hard part.

**Workflow redesign is required, not optional.** AI inserted into an existing workflow without redesigning that workflow around it tends to add a layer of complexity rather than remove one. Workers now have a tool to use *in addition to* their existing process, not *instead of* parts of it. The productivity gains from the tool are offset by the friction of managing it alongside unchanged systems.

**Learning takes time and organizational support.** The productivity gains in the controlled studies emerge when workers have learned how to use the tool effectively — what it is good at, where it fails, how to verify its output. In most SMB deployments, this learning is left entirely to the individual worker, unsupported, without time allocated, without feedback mechanisms. AI use stays shallow and inconsistent.

**The organizational gap is where this practice lives.** Workflow redesign, change management, governance, learning infrastructure — that list is the consulting work. The AI is not the problem. The organizational capacity to absorb and use it is the problem. That is not a technology story. It is a people and process story, which is why this curriculum covers those domains before the technology domain.

### The honest synthesis

AI reliably raises individual task productivity under conditions of appropriate learning, workflow integration, and quality oversight.

AI unreliably raises firm-level productivity under conditions that most SMBs currently have: unsupported individual use, no workflow redesign, no governance, no measurement, no feedback loop.

The gap between those two statements is your practice.

---

**Key takeaway:** AI consistently raises task-level productivity in controlled conditions. It does not consistently raise firm-level productivity in real SMB conditions. The gap is organizational — and closing that gap is the work of this consulting practice.

## Lesson 1A-4: Reading the Data — A Practitioner's Literacy Exercise

**Type:** AI-Assisted Research | **Estimated time:** 20 minutes

---

### The habit this lesson is building

Everything in Module 1A has asked you to read research claims carefully: to distinguish who funded a study, what it actually measured, whether a figure is a projection or an observation, and whether a finding has been replicated. This is not a research methods course. But you do need to be able to look at a commonly cited AI statistic and ask three questions:

1. What is the primary source?
2. Who funded the research?
3. Is this a measured finding or a projected finding?

These questions take about two minutes to apply. The habit of applying them consistently is what separates a practitioner who can hold a grounded position in a client conversation from one who is passing on whatever hype or panic they absorbed from their media feed.

### The exercise

Below are six AI-and-work claims you are likely to encounter in client conversations, pitch meetings, and business media. For each one, use the curriculum AI assistant to trace the claim to its primary source, identify the funder or institutional origin, and classify it as measured or projected.

Work through at least two of the six. If you want to go deeper, work through all six — the patterns across the full set are instructive.

**Claim A:** "AI will create 170 million new jobs and displace 92 million by 2030."

**Claim B:** "Workers using AI complete writing tasks 40% faster."

**Claim C:** "Over 70% of employees are already using AI at work."

**Claim D:** "95% of enterprise AI pilots produce no measurable business impact."

**Claim E:** "AI could add $2.6 to $4.4 trillion in value to the global economy annually."

**Claim F:** "Entry-level employment in AI-exposed occupations is declining for workers under 25."

**How to use the AI assistant:** Ask it to trace the claim to its primary source, identify who funded the research, and tell you whether this is a measured finding or a projection. Then push: ask about the methodology, and whether credible studies challenge or contradict it.

### Debrief questions

After completing the exercise, write brief responses to these three questions:

1. Which claim diverged most from what you expected to find at the primary source — and what was the divergence?
2. Which claims would you feel comfortable citing in a client conversation, with what caveats? Which would you not cite, and why?
3. When you encounter a new AI-and-work claim in the future, what is the minimum check you would run before treating it as reliable?

*There are no model answers. These questions are asking you to articulate your own calibration, which is the point.*

---

**Key takeaway:** Epistemic discipline is a professional skill. The practitioner who can locate a primary source, identify its funder, and distinguish measurement from projection will give clients more accurate guidance than any amount of AI industry fluency.

## Lesson 1B-1: The Five Thriving Markers

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 30 minutes

---

### What the cross-referencing shows

Module 1A established the overall adoption picture. This module asks a more useful question: within that picture, who is doing well, and what do they have in common?

The five markers below are drawn from cross-referencing MIT NANDA's 2025 deployment study, the McKinsey *State of AI 2025* survey, Goldman Sachs SMB pulse surveys from 2024–2025, and BTOS microdata analysis. No single source produces these five cleanly — they emerge from the convergence of multiple independent datasets. That convergence is what makes them reliable enough to use as a diagnostic framework.

These are not guarantees. They are patterns. A firm with all five markers can still fail to realize value from AI, and a firm with none of them can occasionally get lucky. But in aggregate, these markers predict meaningfully. More importantly, they are *actionable* — each one is something a firm can build, not just a trait it either has or doesn't.

### Marker 1: Clear human ownership of AI use decisions

In thriving firms, there is a specific human being — not a vendor, not a committee, not the CEO abstractly — who is responsible for deciding what work gets done with AI, how output gets reviewed, and what happens when AI produces something wrong. This person is not necessarily a technical person. They are an accountable person.

The absence of this marker is more common than its presence. In most SMBs, AI decisions are either made by individual employees without coordination, or they float at the leadership level without a clear owner. The result is inconsistent use, inconsistent quality, and no mechanism for learning from what is and isn't working.

In discovery, you surface this marker by asking: "Who in your organization decides which tasks should use AI tools, and who reviews AI-generated output before it goes to clients or gets used to make decisions?" A confident, specific answer is a positive signal. A pause followed by "well, everyone kind of does their own thing" is a negative one.

### Marker 2: Prior workflow documentation

Thriving firms documented how their core work actually happens before they layered AI on top of it. They have something to integrate AI into. Not necessarily formal SOPs — sometimes it is a checklist, a structured Notion page, or a consistent meeting cadence. The key is that the work was legible before AI arrived.

This matters because AI does not create structure — it amplifies whatever structure (or lack of structure) is already there. A firm whose client onboarding process lives entirely in one person's head cannot automate it; they have to document it first. The firms doing this work before their competitors has a compounding advantage: they can evaluate AI tools against known, measurable workflows rather than vague intuitions about how things go.

In discovery: "Do you have documented processes for your core workflows — client delivery, onboarding, billing, quality review? If I asked a new hire to follow the documented version, could they?" The answers to this question tend to be revealing well beyond the AI question.

### Marker 3: Genuine team communication about what is and is not working

The word "genuine" is doing work in this marker. Most SMBs have meetings. Most owners believe they communicate well with their teams. The research on psychological safety (covered in Module 2B) consistently shows that the owner's perception of communication quality is often significantly more optimistic than the team's experience of it.

What distinguishes thriving firms is that AI-related information actually flows in both directions: employees tell leadership when AI tools are producing bad output, when workflows are not working, when they have found something useful, when they are uncomfortable with something. This requires safety, not just access. Teams that fear their AI-related disclosures might be used against them — to justify role elimination, for instance — stop disclosing.

In discovery, you cannot assess this marker directly by asking the owner. You assess it by asking the team, separately, the same questions. The gap between the two accounts is the data.

### Marker 4: Selective, specific AI adoption

Thriving firms use fewer AI tools for more defined purposes. They chose a specific tool for a specific problem — AI-assisted transcription for client calls, AI-assisted first drafts for a specific document type, AI-assisted data extraction from a specific report format — and they defined what success looks like before they started.

This is the inverse of the common pattern, which is adopting many tools because they seem useful or because a vendor was persuasive, and then using each of them inconsistently for loosely defined purposes.

Selectivity is not timidity. It is discipline. The firms making the most measurable progress are not the ones with the broadest AI portfolios — they are the ones with the clearest criteria for what they are trying to accomplish and the willingness to stop using something that is not working.

In discovery: "How did you decide to adopt [specific tool]? What were you trying to accomplish? How do you know if it's working?" The answer to "how do you know if it's working" is particularly revealing. Most firms do not have an answer.

### Marker 5: Active reskilling investment, even modest in scale

Thriving firms have done something — sometimes very small — to help their people build capability with AI tools rather than just deploying the tools and hoping. This might be two hours a month of team learning time. It might be one person designated as the internal AI-curious explorer who teaches others informally. It might be a shared Slack channel where people post things that did and didn't work.

The scale is less important than the intentionality. What thriving firms have in common is that someone made a decision to invest in learning, rather than leaving learning to happen accidentally. The research on AI productivity gains consistently shows that the gains are not automatic — they require learning time, and that learning time has to be allocated rather than assumed.

In discovery: "What are you doing to help your team get better at working with AI tools?" is a simple and useful question. The range of answers is wide, and the quality of the answer tells you something important about the firm's readiness for any change you are about to recommend.

---

**Key takeaway:** The five thriving markers — human ownership, prior documentation, genuine communication, selective adoption, and deliberate reskilling — are patterns, not prescriptions. Each one is something a firm can build. Your job in discovery is to find out which ones are present and which aren't.

## Lesson 1B-2: The Four Struggling Markers

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 20 minutes

---

### What struggling looks like

Just as the thriving markers appear with consistent frequency in high-performing SMBs, four characteristics appear with consistent frequency in firms that are not realizing value from AI and are accumulating fragility as a result. These are structural patterns, not individual failures. They tend to develop for understandable reasons — competitive pressure, resource constraints, the pace of change — and the practitioner's job is to understand them as systems problems, not blame structures.

### Marker 1: Reactive, vendor-led adoption

The firm is adopting AI tools primarily in response to competitive anxiety ("our competitors are using it"), vendor persuasion ("this sales pitch was convincing"), or media pressure ("we read we need to be doing this") — rather than in response to a diagnosed workflow problem.

Reactive adoption produces a predictable outcome: the firm has tools it does not fully use, solving problems it did not clearly define, with no measurement framework to evaluate whether anything improved. It also tends to produce tool sprawl — because the next vendor is always persuasive, and the previous tools are not being evaluated rigorously enough to be eliminated.

This marker is not the same as being slow or conservative. A firm that waits longer but adopts more deliberately is in a stronger position than a firm that moves fast for fear of being left behind. The speed-versus-caution framing is a vendor narrative. The real variable is intentionality.

### Marker 2: Undocumented processes that AI is expected to fix

The firm has real operational problems — inconsistent client delivery, founder bottlenecks, scaling difficulty — and has decided that AI is the solution to those problems. The processes themselves remain undocumented. The hope is that AI will impose order on chaos.

It will not. This is the single most consistent finding across the NANDA study, BCG deployment research, and practitioner experience: AI does not create structure, it amplifies existing structure. Deploying AI on top of an undocumented process produces a faster, more automated version of the same inconsistency. Sometimes it produces the inconsistency at greater scale and with more confident-sounding output, which makes it harder to catch.

The firms where AI has failed most visibly at the SMB level are often firms where the pre-existing process problem was misdiagnosed as a technology problem. The technology was not the constraint. Documentation, accountability, and workflow clarity were the constraints. AI did not fix those; it obscured them temporarily and then made them worse.

### Marker 3: A leadership communication vacuum about workforce implications

The firm's leadership has not told the team anything clear and honest about what AI adoption means for their jobs. The communication vacuum is filled by rumor, anxiety, and assumption — and the assumptions tend to be worse than the reality.

This vacuum is usually not malicious. Most SMB owners genuinely do not know what AI adoption means for their team's roles. They are uncertain themselves. But the organizational consequence of that uncertainty communicated as silence is significant: teams that do not have clear information about workforce implications tend to underperform during AI transitions, because they are spending cognitive and emotional energy on job security concerns rather than on the learning and experimentation the transition requires.

The safety paradox — which Module 2B covers in depth — is the specific version of this problem that is hardest to address: the owner who is running undisclosed workforce planning while simultaneously asking the team to be honest about AI use. It is worth flagging here because it appears frequently and because the consequences of mishandling it are severe.

### Marker 4: Tool sprawl without integration or governance

The firm has accumulated AI-flavored SaaS subscriptions — often 8–20 or more — with overlapping functions, no integration between them, no governance about which is authoritative, and no systematic evaluation of which ones are actually being used. The subscriptions continue because nobody has audited them, and cancelling something feels risky even when nobody uses it.

Tool sprawl is not just a budget problem, though at $10–100 per tool per month across 10–20 tools, it is also a budget problem. It is a cognitive load problem: every tool the firm nominally uses is a context switch that has to happen, a password that has to be managed, a workflow that has to be checked. It is a data governance problem: client and business data is scattered across systems with no clear ownership. And it is a distraction from the real question, which is not "which of our 15 tools should we use for this?" but "what is the smallest set of well-integrated tools that reliably produces the outcome we need?"

In discovery, the technology audit (Domain 4) exists to surface and evaluate this comprehensively. At the first-contact stage, a simple question suffices: "How many AI or AI-assisted tools are you currently paying for? Can you name them all?" The inability to name them all is itself diagnostic.

---

**Key takeaway:** The four struggling markers — reactive adoption, undocumented processes, communication vacuum, and tool sprawl — are structural patterns that develop for understandable reasons. They are not failures of intelligence or intention. They are systems problems with systems solutions.

## Lesson 1B-3: The 15-Minute First-Contact Diagnostic

**Type:** Simulation | **Estimated time:** 30 minutes

---

### What this is and what it is not

The nine markers from Lessons 1B-1 and 1B-2 are not a scorecard. You are not going to hand a new client a rubric and ask them to rate themselves on five dimensions. You are going to have a conversation — usually 15–30 minutes, usually on a first call or at the beginning of a first meeting — and the nine markers are what you are listening for while that conversation happens.

The diagnostic identifies starting-point hypotheses to investigate. It does not produce verdicts to deliver. A firm that appears to have three struggling markers in the first conversation may turn out, on deeper investigation, to have significant strengths that were not visible in that first exchange. A firm that sounds like it has four thriving markers may have presented a carefully polished version of itself that does not survive contact with the team. The first contact is a beginning, not a conclusion.

What the diagnostic does is sharpen your listening and guide your next questions. It keeps you from walking out of a first conversation with a vague general impression ("they seem pretty organized") and walking in with a specific set of hypotheses about what is and is not present and what you need to find out next.

### The question map

For each marker, there is a question — or a category of question — that tends to open it. These are not scripts. They are categories of inquiry you are holding while a conversation happens.

**Human ownership:** "Who in your organization is responsible for AI decisions — what gets used, how it's reviewed, what happens when it goes wrong?" Listen for: a specific name and role, or a vague collective ("we all kind of figure it out together").

**Prior documentation:** "If I asked a new hire to follow your documented process for [core workflow], could they do it from the documentation alone?" Listen for: "yes, we have a runbook" versus "well, they'd probably shadow someone" versus "we'd need to explain it to them."

**Team communication:** "When something isn't working with an AI tool — bad output, awkward integration, something that's creating more work — how does that get surfaced and addressed?" Listen for: a specific mechanism, or absence of one.

**Selective adoption:** "When you adopted [specific AI tool], what workflow problem were you trying to solve, and how did you know whether it worked?" Listen for: a clear before/after or success criterion, or "we just wanted to try it."

**Reskilling:** "What are you doing to help your team get better at working with AI?" Listen for: any intentional investment, however small, or nothing beyond the tool deployment itself.

**Reactive adoption (struggling marker):** "What prompted you to start using AI tools?" Listen for: competitor anxiety, vendor pressure, media, versus a specific internal problem that needed solving.

**Undocumented processes (struggling marker):** "Where do your core processes live? Can you point me to documentation, or does the knowledge live with specific people?" Listen for: "we have documentation" versus "it's mostly in [person]'s head."

**Communication vacuum (struggling marker):** "Have you talked with your team directly about what AI adoption means for their roles?" Listen for: a specific, honest account of what was said, or "we haven't really gotten into that yet."

**Tool sprawl (struggling marker):** "How many AI or AI-flavored tools are you currently paying for? Can you name them?" Listen for: a complete, confident list versus "probably around [number], I'd have to check."

## Lesson 1B-4: The Misread — Adoption as Readiness

**Type:** Reading + Reflection | **Estimated time:** 10 minutes

### The most common early-stage error

You will encounter SMBs that use many AI tools. Some of them will talk fluently about AI in their work. Owners will mention specific tools by name, describe use cases with apparent confidence, and give the impression of a firm that has its AI act together.

This is the misread: interpreting the number of AI tools a firm uses, or the fluency with which its owner talks about AI, as a measure of strategic readiness.

It is not. In fact, there is a modest inverse relationship in the data: firms with the broadest AI tool portfolios tend to have the least governance, because breadth is often a symptom of reactive, unsystematic adoption — the same market anxiety that produces tool sprawl produces AI vocabulary. A firm using fifteen AI tools with no governance, no documented processes, no team communication about workforce implications, and no success metrics is more fragile than a firm using one AI tool with a clear owner, a documented workflow, and a measurement approach — even if the one-tool firm sounds, in a first conversation, less sophisticated.

The practitioner who gets dazzled by the tool count or the vocabulary will misdiagnose the engagement. They will treat a governance and process problem as an opportunity for more technology, which is the opposite of what the firm needs.

Think about a firm — real or hypothetical — that presents as AI-forward: lots of tools, fluent leadership, active experimentation. Now apply the five thriving markers. How many of them actually appear? What does that tell you about what the firm actually needs from an engagement?

## Lesson 1C-1: Anxious Pragmatism — Using AI While Fearing It

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 25 minutes

---

### What the data shows about workers right now

The American Psychological Association's *Work in America* surveys have tracked worker attitudes toward AI across three annual waves (2023, 2024, 2025). The consistent finding: the share of U.S. workers who report worrying that AI will make some or all of their job duties obsolete has risen year over year. By 2024, the figure was approximately 38% of all U.S. workers. Among knowledge workers — which includes essentially every employee at the SMBs you will work with — the proportion is higher.

At the same time, those same workers reported using AI tools more than the year before.

Anxiety and use are rising together. This is the core phenomenon: workers who fear their jobs may be at risk from AI are simultaneously adopting AI tools to do their work more effectively. They are pragmatic about the tool's utility. They are anxious about its long-term implications for their employment. Both things are true at once, often inside the same person on the same day.

The Pew Research Center (2023, 2024, 2025 waves) has consistently found that the U.S. public views AI in the workplace with more concern than excitement by roughly a 2:1 margin, and that this gap is widening — not closing — as familiarity increases. People who understand AI better are not reliably more comfortable with it. They are sometimes less comfortable, because they understand more specifically what it can do.

Gallup's *State of the Global Workplace 2025* added a layer to this picture: global employee engagement — the share of workers who report feeling committed to and energized by their work — declined to 21% from 23% the prior year. This is the first sustained decline in over a decade. Gallup attributes part of it to a sense of accelerating change without commensurate organizational support. Managers showed the sharpest engagement drop, which is consistent with the position they are in: responsible for managing team members through AI transitions they were not trained for, while simultaneously uncertain about their own roles.

### Why this matters for what information you get

Anxious pragmatism produces a specific information problem for the practitioner: people will not tell you what they are actually afraid of, and will not tell you the full extent of their unauthorized AI use.

The fear operates on two levels simultaneously. Workers who are afraid that AI might eliminate their role are also afraid that disclosing their AI use — the shortcuts they have found, the tasks they have handed off, the parts of their job they no longer do manually — might accelerate that elimination. If they show leadership that AI can do what they do, have they made themselves redundant?

The rational response to this fear is to underreport. To say "we tried some tools but didn't really find them useful" while actually using them daily. To say "I don't really use AI in my work" while using it on every third task. This is not dishonesty in the usual sense. It is self-protection under conditions of genuine uncertainty.

The practitioner who walks into a discovery session expecting honest, complete disclosure about AI use and AI anxiety will not get it, unless they have created the conditions in which honest disclosure is safe. What those conditions look like — and how to build them — is the subject of Module 2B. For now, the essential understanding is this: the climate you are walking into is one of anxious pragmatism, and the information it produces will be systematically incomplete in predictable directions.

### The BYOAI layer

The Microsoft/LinkedIn Work Trend Index 2024 found that 75% of global knowledge workers use generative AI at work, with 78% of those users bringing their own tools without employer approval. The 2025 follow-up showed the pattern persisting.

Employees bring their own AI accounts to work for the same reasons they bring their own phones: the tools are available, they are useful, and the employer has not provided or prohibited an alternative. The worker's calculation is local and pragmatic. The organizational implications — data governance, quality control, role definition — are invisible from where they sit.

This means that the team you discover in a client engagement is very likely using AI in ways neither the leadership nor you know about yet. The question is not whether this is happening. The question is what it is happening with, on what tasks, with what results, and what it reveals about where the organization's real workflow pain and real workflow opportunity live.

---

**Key takeaway:** Workers are adopting AI while fearing it. This produces systematic underreporting of both AI use and AI anxiety. The practitioner who does not account for this will build recommendations on incomplete information.

## Lesson 1C-2: The Neuroscience of Threat — Why Fear Impairs Learning

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 20 minutes

---

### What happens to thinking under threat

When a person perceives their job as genuinely threatened — not hypothetically threatened, actually threatened, with real evidence to support that perception — their brain does something predictable and unhelpful: it activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the same physiological stress response system that evolved to handle physical threats. Cortisol and adrenaline are released. Blood flow shifts. Cognitive resources are reallocated toward threat monitoring and away from the prefrontal cortex functions that support creative thinking, learning, complex reasoning, and behavioral flexibility.

This is Robert Sapolsky's core argument in *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers*, applied to human organizational life: the stress response that served us well for acute physical threats does real damage when it is chronically activated by social and economic threats. The physiology was designed for short-term emergencies; chronic job insecurity is not a short-term emergency.

For the practitioner, the practical implication is significant: teams who fear AI-related job displacement are cognitively less able to learn the new tools they are being asked to adopt. The fear of being replaced by a tool impairs the learning of that tool. This is not resistance. It is not stubbornness. It is physiology, and the practitioner who treats it as a cultural or attitude problem will try to solve it with the wrong tools.

### The SCARF vocabulary

David Rock's SCARF model (NeuroLeadership Institute, 2008 and subsequent updates) offers a useful vocabulary — not a psychometric instrument, not a validated scale, but a vocabulary — for understanding why people behave the way they do in organizational change environments.

SCARF identifies five domains in which perceived threat produces diminished performance and prosocial behavior: Status (perceived standing relative to others), Certainty (ability to predict the future), Autonomy (sense of control over one's situation), Relatedness (sense of connection and safety with others), and Fairness (perception that outcomes are equitable).

An AI transition threatens all five simultaneously for many workers: it may lower status if the worker is seen as less relevant; it eliminates certainty about future role requirements; it reduces autonomy if algorithms start governing work decisions; it may damage relatedness if coworkers are competing for diminished positions; and it often feels unfair in its distribution of risk and benefit.

The SCARF framing is useful as practitioner vocabulary — a way of thinking about what specifically is activating threat response in a given team — not as a diagnostic instrument to apply rigorously. Its validity as a formal psychometric tool is contested in academic literature. Use it to think, not to score.

### The direct implication for your work

Two things follow from this for how you operate in client engagements.

First: if you walk into an organization where staff are experiencing genuine threat response around AI, the quality of information you get from them will be degraded in predictable ways. They will be more likely to tell you what they think is safe to say, less likely to volunteer problems, less likely to engage creatively with new ideas, and more likely to present a defensive, managed version of their experience. This is not personality — it is biology responding to real conditions.

Second: any recommendation you make that requires the team to learn new things, try new approaches, or change established patterns is going to face a different kind of friction than the usual organizational change friction if threat response is active. Learning requires psychological safety. The safety has to come first. This is the direct reason Domain 2 begins with psychological safety before it touches any AI-specific content.

---

**Key takeaway:** Fear of job displacement is not a cultural attitude problem — it is a physiological state that measurably impairs the learning and flexibility required to navigate AI transitions. The practitioner who understands this will not try to solve it with enthusiasm campaigns.

## Lesson 1C-3: The BYOAI Signal

**Type:** Reading + Reflection | **Estimated time:** 20 minutes

---

### Reframing what you are looking at

When workers use personal AI accounts for work tasks without employer knowledge, most organizations respond in one of two ways: ignoring it as unimportant, or treating it as a security and compliance problem to be locked down. Both responses miss the more useful frame.

BYOAI is a signal. It tells you four things about the organization you are in.

**Where the real workflow friction is.** Employees use shadow AI for the same reason people use workarounds in general: because the official way of doing something is too slow, too complicated, or produces inadequate results. The tasks where BYOAI concentrates are the tasks where friction is highest. Those are the tasks your process audit should prioritize, and they are often not the tasks leadership thinks are the biggest problems.

**Where AI actually adds value in this specific organization.** Rather than theorizing about where AI might help, you can observe where employees have already found help. The tools they chose, the tasks they applied them to, and the frequency of use are real data about real value delivered in the real context of this organization. This is more useful than any vendor case study.

**Where governance gaps are — framed as design opportunities.** The gap between "employees are using AI tools we do not control, review, or govern" and "employees have a clear, well-designed AI workflow with appropriate oversight" is a gap the practitioner can close. The BYOAI finding tells you the gap exists and gives you a starting point for what governance needs to address.

**Which employees are already ahead.** The workers who have found shadow AI tools and integrated them into their work are the first adopters, the internal champions, the people most likely to help build something better if they are brought into the process rather than corrected. Knowing who they are is valuable.

### The security dimension — held in context

BYOAI does carry real data security and compliance risk. Client data shared with free-tier AI accounts. Confidential business information processed by third-party systems. Output used in client deliverables without review. These are genuine risks and the technology audit (Module 4C) addresses them directly.

The point here is sequencing: the security conversation is more productive after you have understood what the BYOAI is telling you about workflow, value, and governance needs. An organization that locks down AI tools without addressing the underlying workflow friction has simply driven the shadow adoption deeper and added a layer of employee resentment. The better sequence is understand the signal, then design the solution.

### Reflection prompt

Consider the BYOAI pattern at a firm you know. If you were doing discovery and found that three employees were regularly using personal ChatGPT accounts for client-facing work, what are the three most important things you would want to understand before making any recommendation? What would you not do first?

---

**Key takeaway:** BYOAI is a map of where workflow friction is real, where AI value is already being captured informally, and where governance needs to be designed. Treat it as intelligence before treating it as a compliance problem.

## Lesson 1D-1: The Major Projections — What They Actually Say

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 25 minutes

---

### Why this lesson exists

Client conversations in 2026 are dominated by macro projections. The World Economic Forum says 22% of jobs will be structurally disrupted by 2030. McKinsey estimates AI could add $2.6 to $4.4 trillion in annual economic value. Goldman Sachs has estimated that AI could automate 25% of work tasks in the U.S. Your clients have read some version of these numbers, and they have shaped the conversations you are walking into.

The practitioner's job is not to dismiss these projections, and it is not to accept them uncritically. The job is to understand what kind of claim each one actually is — what methodology produced it, what assumptions it embeds, and what the honest uncertainty range looks like — so you can help clients make decisions based on something better than the headline.

### WEF Future of Jobs 2025

The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report* is one of the most cited documents in conversations about AI and work. The 2025 edition projected that 22% of current jobs would be structurally disrupted by 2030, with 170 million new roles created and 92 million displaced for a net gain of 78 million.

The methodology: employer surveys. The WEF asked HR leaders and senior executives at large firms what they expected to change about their workforces over the next five years. It is a survey of anticipated future decisions, not an observation of current or past behavior.

This is important because employer forecasts of workforce change have a poor historical track record. Firms consistently overestimate how quickly they will automate and underestimate how long change management takes. The WEF figures tell you what employers are currently thinking about the future. They do not tell you what the future will actually look like.

The 40% skills-update claim — that 40% of current worker skills will need to be updated by 2027 — comes from the same survey methodology and carries the same caveats. It is a claim about employer expectations, not a measurement of skill obsolescence.

### McKinsey productivity scenarios

McKinsey Global Institute has published several analyses of AI's potential economic impact, with headline figures in the range of $2.6 to $4.4 trillion in annual value. These figures come from scenario modeling: economists build models of how AI might affect productivity across different industries, under different assumptions about adoption rates, implementation quality, and enabling conditions.

Scenario models are valuable for thinking about ranges of possible futures. They are not forecasts. The $2.6 trillion figure assumes a relatively slow, uneven adoption trajectory; the $4.4 trillion figure assumes a faster, more uniform one. The actual outcome will depend on factors — organizational capacity, regulation, labor market adjustment, technological development — that the model cannot resolve.

The McKinsey figures are often cited as if they describe what AI will do. They describe what AI might do under specified assumptions. The assumptions are not always stated in the coverage.

### Goldman Sachs and the displacement estimates

Goldman Sachs published a 2023 report ("The Potentially Large Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Economic Growth") estimating that AI could automate 25% of work tasks in the U.S. and Europe, displacing approximately 300 million full-time equivalent jobs globally. The same report projected significant GDP gains if the productivity benefits were fully realized.

The Goldman report was rigorous by investment bank standards and has been widely cited — and widely criticized. The 25% task-automation estimate rests on a task-level analysis that identifies which tasks are technically automatable, not which tasks will actually be automated under real conditions of implementation, cost, organizational capacity, and worker adaptation. Technically automatable and economically automatable and actually automated are three different things at three different scales of likelihood.

### How to read any projection document

The same four questions apply to every macro projection you encounter:

1. **What is the methodology?** Is this observed behavior, employer surveys about expected behavior, economic scenario modeling, or task-level technical analysis? Each produces a different kind of claim with different reliability characteristics.

2. **Who funded it?** A projection produced by a consulting firm with advisory services to sell in the AI space should be read with that context in mind. Not dismissed — but read with awareness.

3. **What are the key assumptions?** Where is the range of the estimate, and what would have to be true for the optimistic scenario to materialize versus the pessimistic one?

4. **What measured data underlies it?** Projections built on actual observations (payroll data, employment records, productivity measurements) are more reliable than projections built on expected future states.

---

**Key takeaway:** The major AI-and-work projections — WEF, McKinsey, Goldman Sachs — are methodologically distinct from measured findings. They are projections: estimates of possible futures under specified assumptions. The practitioner's job is to understand what methodology produced a figure before citing or rebutting it.

## Lesson 1D-2: The Acemoglu-Restrepo Counter

**Type:** Reading | **Estimated time:** 20 minutes

---

### The argument

MIT economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo have developed the most rigorous empirical counter-argument to productivity optimism about AI and automation. Their framework, developed across several papers from 2018 through 2024–2025, centers on what they call "so-so automation."

The core claim: much automation replaces workers without generating productivity gains large enough to justify the displacement. In a so-so automation scenario, technology eliminates human tasks but does not generate the new tasks, new industries, or productivity growth that would create sufficient replacement work and economic benefit. The net result is displacement without compensation — workers lose work, and society does not gain enough productivity to offset the loss.

Their empirical work on earlier waves of automation (industrial robots in U.S. manufacturing, 1990–2007) found that robot adoption reduced employment and wages in affected commuting zones more than standard models predicted, with limited offsetting job creation. Their more recent work extends this analysis to the question of whether generative AI follows the same pattern.

Their estimate of AI's likely GDP impact over ten years — roughly under 1% — is considerably more conservative than McKinsey's, Goldman's, or the WEF's, and it is based on a fundamentally different analytical premise: that the productivity gains from current AI systems are real but modest, and that the economic conditions required for those gains to compound into large macroeconomic effects are not reliably present.

### Where this framework holds and where it has limits

The Acemoglu-Restrepo argument is important precisely because it takes the evidence seriously and arrives at a conservative conclusion. In a field dominated by either optimistic vendor projections or alarmist displacement narratives, their empirical caution is a useful counterweight.

But it has limits. Their historical analysis of industrial automation may not transfer cleanly to generative AI, which operates differently at the task level and spreads across industries in different patterns than robots in manufacturing. The finding that prior automation was often "so-so" does not prove that current AI will be — though it does provide reasonable grounds for skepticism about the most optimistic projections.

The honest position: Acemoglu-Restrepo is an important corrective to productivity triumphalism. It is not the final word. The macroeconomic effects of generative AI are genuinely uncertain, and the empirical literature is too young to settle the question. That uncertainty is the appropriate thing to communicate to clients.

### What this means at SMB scale

For the SMBs you work with, the macro-level debate is partly beside the point. Whether AI adds 0.5% or 5% to GDP over ten years matters for economists and policymakers. What matters for a 20-person firm is whether the specific AI investments they make generate value for them, in their context, with their team. The so-so automation risk is real at that scale: an SMB can spend significant resources on AI tools and change management and end up with modest productivity gains, worker anxiety, and a set of tools that are partially adopted and imperfectly integrated. That is not a catastrophe, but it is not the transformation they were sold.

The practitioner who has internalized the so-so automation framework will not oversell AI's transformative potential to clients who are afraid of being left behind. They will help clients make specific, evidence-based investments with realistic expectations and clear measurement approaches.

---

**Key takeaway:** The so-so automation framework is an evidence-based counter to AI productivity optimism — not a refutation, but a calibration. Much automation displaces workers without generating offsetting gains. Current generative AI may or may not follow the same pattern, and the honest answer is that we do not yet know.

## Lesson 1D-3: Modeling Uncertainty Without Paralysis

**Type:** Simulation + Reflection | **Estimated time:** 15 minutes

---

### The practitioner's specific challenge

You are not a researcher. You are not an economist. You are someone clients will ask direct questions about the future, and the honest answer to most of those questions is some version of "we don't know yet."

The challenge is how to say that in a way that is useful rather than evasive. A practitioner who hedges everything into uselessness — "well, there are various projections and it's really hard to say" — has not helped the client make better decisions. A practitioner who projects false certainty — "based on the research, your team is going to need to reskill entirely within 18 months" — has given the client a confident feeling built on something that is not true.

There is a third option, and it is the one this curriculum is asking you to develop: communicating real uncertainty in a way that orients rather than paralyzes.

What that looks like: you distinguish what is measured from what is projected. You say what the measured evidence actually shows, with appropriate confidence. You describe the range of projection-level uncertainty honestly. And you bring the client back to what this means for their decisions right now, with the information available right now — which is a more useful place to land than the unresolvable macro-level question.

### Simulation

A client asks you directly: *"Is AI going to eliminate my team's jobs in the next three years?"*

Write the response you would give. Then review it against these questions:

- Did you give a false sense of certainty in either direction?
- Did you hedge so much that the client is no better equipped to make a decision?
- Did you distinguish measured findings from projections?
- Did you bring the conversation to something the client can actually act on?
- Does the client leave this exchange better oriented, or more confused?

Revise if needed.

---

**Key takeaway:** Communicating genuine uncertainty without paralysis is a professional skill — not a rhetorical one. It requires knowing what is measured and what is projected, being honest about the difference, and helping clients make good decisions with the information that actually exists.
